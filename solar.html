When SWPC states a chance of a solar flare or a geomagnetic storm, how often does it happen?

Every day at 2200 UTC the NOAA Space Weather Prediction Center issues its Report and Forecast of Solar and Geophysical Activity. It states a chance for each of the next three days. The product description defines them. Class M and class X are a “probability forecast of the occurrence of one or more class-M or class-X x-ray flares for each of the next 3 days”. The geomagnetic chances are “the probability of at least one 3-hour K index, at the indicated level” at middle and high latitudes, with minor storm at K = 5 and major or severe storm at K of 6 or more. If a stated 30 percent holds, the event should come on about 30 percent of such days.

The stated chances are read from the reports as first published, kept in the SWPC Warehouse, and from copies this desk stores. A corrected or amended report is never used, and a forecast counts only if it was issued before its day began. What happened is read from SWPC’s own records: the edited solar event lists for flares, and the daily geomagnetic data for the eight 3-hour K values a day at Fredericksburg and at College. The report does not name its middle and high latitude stations. Reading them as Fredericksburg and College, the stations SWPC lists under those headings, is our reading. An M-class day is a day with a flare of M1.0 or more at its peak, and reading Class M as M or greater is also ours.

The frame starts with forecasts issued on 1 January 2021, more than a year after SWPC moved its main X-ray input to GOES-16 and stopped scaling flare classes, so every day is on one flare scale. Scored days run from 2021-01-02 to 2026-11-06. All four known-good checks passed on this run. The plan, with the bins, events and rules, was fixed before any stated chance was set beside an outcome. One addendum was made after the first read and is marked where it applies.

A bin is a range of stated chance. Above means the event came more often than the mean stated chance in the bin, and below means less often, each by more than the 95% Wilson interval. Consistent means the mean stated chance is inside the interval. A bin with fewer than 30 days reads cannot tell. The Brier score is the mean squared gap between the stated chance and what happened. The skill compares it with always stating the base rate of the same days. Above zero is better than the base rate, and the skill reads consistent if its whole interval is within 0.05 of zero.

The Wilson interval treats days as independent. They are not. Active regions and storms last for days, and the three days of one forecast share most of what the forecaster knew, so the Wilson interval can be too narrow. The effect is not the same everywhere. It is largest for M-class flares and high latitude storms, where the interval may be about one and a half times too narrow, and small for X-class flares and middle latitude storms. The skill interval is a moving block bootstrap over blocks of 27 days, one solar rotation, which keeps runs of days together. Each bin table also shows a block bootstrap interval beside the Wilson interval, as a description. The plan’s reading uses the Wilson interval.

Proton events are held

The report also states a chance of a proton event, at least 10 pfu above 10 MeV. The plan scored it against the SWPC list of solar proton events. That list leaves out events SWPC’s own daily reports record. The report of 23 March 2024 says protons above 10 MeV “reached a peak level of 955 pfu”, and the list has no event in March 2024. Scored against that list a stated chance would look too high, so the proton rows read cannot tell. Their figures stay in the data, marked as held, until a complete record is set in a later plan.

What this page holds and what it leaves out

The delivered records are SWPC’s preliminary records. SWPC says it does not encourage the use of preliminary data for research. The edited event lists are read once a day has been over for at least three days. A flare that begins before midnight and peaks after it counts on the day of its peak. SWPC’s Daily Solar Data counts it on the day it began, and the two records agree on whether a day had an M-class or larger flare on 1,810 of 1,821 days. Days with no report, no event list or a missing K value are counted apart and are never read as days with or without an event. Reports refused as a correction or amendment, or that did not parse: 2.

Set aside: the 3-day forecast and the 3-day geomagnetic forecast, which SWPC keeps for 75 days only. This desk stores each new one so a later plan can score them. Also set aside: the polar cap absorption forecast, flux and Ap predictions, and forecasts before 2021. A bin that reads above or below says the stated chance and the share of days differ for this record and this method. This page does not say why.
