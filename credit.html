CREDIT · Stated Complaint Responses and Card Terms Against the Public Record

99.55%

In 2025 the CFPB printed that companies gave a timely response to 99.6% of complaints sent to them. In the public complaint database, 5,418,414 of 5,442,927 complaints received that year are flagged timely, 99.55 percent with a 95% interval of 99.35 to 99.68 percent, and the reading is cannot tell. The three nationwide credit bureaus: Equifax 0 not timely of 1,680,537, Experian 32 not timely of 1,388,821 and TransUnion 0 of 1,601,849. Of the 115 companies with at least 1,000 complaints across at least 10 months of 2025, 84 had a not timely share below the share the printed figure leaves, 15 above it and 16 cannot tell.

Division: HERMES

When the CFPB states how companies answer complaints, what does its own database show?

The Consumer Financial Protection Bureau sends consumer complaints to the companies named in them and publishes the complaints in its Consumer Complaint Database. Its complaint page says: “Companies generally respond in 15 days.” and “In some cases, the company will let you know their response is in progress and provide a final response in 60 days.” Its annual reports define the database flag: “Complaints in which a company did not provide a response within 15 calendar days of the complaint being sent to the company—or within 60 days if it requested more time—are reflected in the Consumer Complaint Database as not having received a timely response.”

Each annual report prints one share for all companies. The 2025 report says companies were “providing a timely response to 99.6% of the more than 5.9 million complaints sent to them for review in 2025.” The 2024 report says 99.7%, the 2023 report 99.6%, and the 2022 and 2021 reports 99%. The 2025 report also says: “Of those complaints sent to companies, the Bureau sent 97% to a company in a day or less.”

This page sets those printed figures beside the database itself, complaints received from 1 January 2021 to 31 December 2025, the last five complete years. It reads the database file of 2026-11-10 (10,765,407 complaints in the window). It names companies as the database names them and uses company-level counts only. No complaint text, ZIP code, state or detail about any person is read into this page.

A printed figure such as 99.6 percent is read as the band its rounding allows, here 99.55 to 99.65 percent. The printed figure counts every complaint sent to companies that year. The database holds the complaints published by the download date, which is fewer (for 2025, 5,442,927 in the database against more than 5.9 million sent). So the two bases differ, and the page prints both counts. The 2025 report says: “Complaints closed with an Alerted CFPB response are not published in the Consumer Complaint Database.” Alerted CFPB is one of its administrative responses. The 2023 and 2024 reports cite this database for their count of complaints not given a timely response. If every complaint sent and not published had a timely response, the timely share on the printed count would sit inside the printed band in each year, as the table shows. So a cannot tell here comes from the width of the interval.

Complaints cluster by company and month. One company's handling or one wave of filings moves many complaints at once. Every interval here is a 95 percent interval from a bootstrap that draws whole company-months, 2,000 draws. It does not cover a wave that moves many companies in the same month. Each cell draws with the plan's seed joined to a checksum of the cell's name, so a cell gives the same interval on every run. A reading whose interval ends within 0.01 points of a band edge is marked at the edge, and another seed can move it. All known-good checks passed on this run. On the first run the rule check failed and every reading showed cannot tell. Its planted test drew one cell and asked that the interval hold the planted share, which one draw can miss by chance. The test now asks that 34 of 40 planted cells hold it, and the readings below come from the run after that change. The plan, with the rules, the cells and the bands, was committed before any printed figure was set beside the database.

The thirty-day reinvestigation period

The Fair Credit Reporting Act, 15 U.S.C. 1681i(a)(1)(A), says that when a consumer disputes an item and “notifies the agency directly, or indirectly through a reseller, of such dispute, the agency shall, free of charge, conduct a reasonable reinvestigation” and finish it “before the end of the 30-day period beginning on the date on which the agency receives the notice of the dispute from the consumer or reseller.” Subparagraph (B) allows “not more than 15 additional days” when the consumer sends relevant information during the period.

The same section, 1681i(e)(3), covers complaints the Bureau sends on to the nationwide bureaus. Each bureau shall “review each such complaint to determine whether all legal obligations imposed on the consumer reporting agency under this subchapter ... have been met”, “provide reports on a regular basis to the Bureau” and keep records. It sets no number of days.

Reading: cannot tell. The database records the date the CFPB received the complaint and the date it sent it to the company. It records no date of company response, and no date a bureau received a dispute notice under 15 U.S.C. 1681i. A complaint to the CFPB is not by itself the dispute notice the statute counts from. So the database cannot show whether any reinvestigation finished within 30 days. Two things it does hold are shown below and decide nothing: how many days the CFPB took to send each bureau its credit reporting complaints, and the share of those complaints the CFPB flagged not timely under its own 15 and 60 day rule.

Card plan APRs beside the Federal Reserve averages

Every six months the CFPB's Terms of Credit Card Plans survey publishes the plans card issuers report, one row per plan, with the purchase APRs each plan states. The Federal Reserve's G.19 release prints two card rates for commercial banks each quarter. In its words, “the rate for all accounts is the stated APR averaged across all credit card accounts at all reporting banks” and “the rate for accounts assessed interest is the annualized ratio of total finance charges at all reporting banks to the total average daily balances against which the finance charges were assessed”.

Reading: cannot tell, not like for like. The survey states APRs offered on plans, one row per plan with no weight for how many accounts it holds, and it includes credit unions and smaller issuers. G.19 averages APRs held on all accounts at reporting commercial banks, weighted by account or by balance. The two are set side by side and no reading is drawn. The G.19 rate on accounts assessed interest falls inside the 10th to 90th percentile of the top 25 issuers' plan medians in every half year with that flag. The all accounts rate falls below the 10th percentile in 2024 H2, 2025 H1 and 2025 H2 and inside it in the other half years (shown only).
