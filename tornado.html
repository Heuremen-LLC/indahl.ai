17.6%

Where the Storm Prediction Center drew a 10 to 15 percent chance of a tornado within 25 miles in its 1300 UTC Day 1 outlook from 2007 to 2025, a tornado came within 25 miles of a point 17.6 percent of the time, with a 95% interval of 16.2 to 19.0 percent, and the band reads above. Of the eight stated bands, 3 consistent, 1 above, 1 cannot tell and 3 with fewer than 30 days, too thin to read. In Kansas the same band held 15.5 percent, from 11.9 to 19.7 percent, over 96 days, and reads cannot tell.

Division: HERMES

When the Storm Prediction Center draws a tornado chance, how often does a tornado come?

Every morning the NOAA Storm Prediction Center issues a Day 1 Convective Outlook. It draws tornado probability contours at 2, 5, 10, 15, 30, 45 and 60 percent. Its product page says the probabilities “represent the probability of one or more events occurring” “within 25 miles of a point during the outlook period.” The Center's verification paper by Kay and Brooks describes the hatched area as a “10% or greater chance of 1 or more F2 or stronger tornadoes”, now rated EF2 or stronger.

This page scores the 1300 UTC Day 1 outlook, valid until 1200 UTC the next day. It uses the 80 km grid spacing and the outlook valid period of the Center's verification paper, which scored the 1300 and 2000 UTC outlooks and binned reports into grid boxes rather than measuring distance to a point. Points 80 km apart cover the 48 contiguous states. None falls in the District of Columbia. Each point takes the highest contour around it, with no smoothing between contours. A point counts as hit when a tornado track in the SPC tornado database passes within 25 miles of it during the outlook period. A point inside the 10 percent contour and outside the 15 percent contour was given a chance from 10 up to 15 percent, and the observed share at those points should sit in that band. That band reading is the one the plan fixed. Read against the contour value alone, with the same interval, every United States contour with 30 or more days shows tornadoes more often than the value drawn, and the table shows that reading beside the band. It was added after the data at the outside referee and decides nothing.

Points on one day share the same storms, and nearby days share weather systems. The intervals come from a bootstrap that draws whole days, so points on the same day stay together. It does not cover the link between neighbouring days. Resampling whole years instead widens the United States 10 to 15 percent interval to 15.1 to 20.2 percent, and the reading stays above. The plan, with the rules, the cells and the known-good checks, was fixed and committed before any outlook was set beside a tornado. All known-good checks passed on this run.

The hatched area and a second ruler

The hatched area states a 10 percent or greater chance of a tornado rated EF2 or stronger within 25 miles. The Brier skill score compares the outlook with climatology, the share of days with a tornado within 25 miles of the same point in the same month in the other closed years. Above zero means the outlook scored better than climatology. The Center states no figure for this score, so it reads only above or below zero. In the closed years 1,546 tornadoes in the database carry no rating, and they are not counted as EF2 or stronger. Unrated tornadoes are rare before 2016 and are 16 percent in 2021, 13 percent in 2022, 20 percent in 2023, 14 percent in 2024, 17 percent in 2025. If every unrated tornado were EF2 or stronger, the hatched share would be at most 10.5 percent in the United States, 7.0 in Kansas and 9.1 in Tornado Alley, so the Kansas and Tornado Alley readings depend on how unrated tornadoes are read.

Do tornado warnings meet their stated goals?

The National Weather Service reports three tornado warning measures each fiscal year under the Government Performance and Results Act. The Commerce Inspector General's report OIG-25-026-I (2025) gives the definitions and the goals it calls current: probability of detection, the mean share of each tornado's one-minute segments that fell inside a warning in force, at least 72 percent. Lead time, the mean over each tornado's segments of the time since the warning was issued, with an unwarned segment counted as zero, at least 13 minutes. False alarm ratio, the share of warnings that no tornado verified, at most 71 percent. The same report says the public description of lead time differs from the one computed. The computed one is used here. The lead time of the first segment is shown beside it and decides nothing.

Warnings are the storm-based tornado warning polygons as issued, from the Iowa Environmental Mesonet archive. Tornadoes are the tracks in the SPC tornado database, cut into one-minute segments along a straight line from start to end. Each fiscal year is read against the current goals. The goals printed for earlier years in each budget were not read. The intervals come from the same day bootstrap. Each issuance polygon is held until the warning expires. The archive also gives the time a polygon was replaced by a later statement, and the replacement polygons are not in the file read. Counting the issuance polygon only until it was replaced gives a floor: detection 34.7 percent, lead 2.6 minutes and false alarms 78.2 percent over the closed years. Detection and lead read below at both ends. The false alarm reading is the most open of the three. When several warnings cover a segment, its lead is taken from the earliest issued.

What this page holds and what it leaves out

Left out: the other Day 1 issuances, the Day 2 and Day 3 outlooks, the hail, wind and categorical outlooks, tornado watches, outlooks before 2007, and Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico in the outlook grid. A reading says how often tornadoes came where a band was drawn. It does not say why.
