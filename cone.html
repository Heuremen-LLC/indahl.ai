When the National Hurricane Center draws a cone, how often does the storm stay inside it?

Every official track forecast from the National Hurricane Center (NHC) comes with a cone. The NHC cone page says the size of each circle that forms it “is set so that two-thirds of historical official forecast errors over a 5-year sample fall within the circle.” The page shown with each forecast says: “Historical data indicate that the entire 5-day path of the center of the tropical cyclone will remain within the cone about 60-70% of the time.” NHC changes the circle sizes every year and prints them in a table.

This page sets each official forecast position from NHC's public forecast files beside the best track, NHC's own post-season record of where the center was, every six hours. It asks two things. Did the whole five-day path stay inside that forecast's cone? And at each lead time, did the center fall inside the circle of that year's size? Seasons 2010 to 2025 (2012 left out, no cone radii were found for it) are read with the post-season best track. The plan, with the rules and the known-good checks, was fixed before any forecast was set beside a best-track position. All four known-good checks passed on this run.

A forecast is scored when it carries all seven lead times and the storm was still a tropical or subtropical cyclone in the best track at every six-hour point from 12 to 120 hours. The cone is built from NHC's circles at 12, 24, 36, 48, 72, 96 and 120 hours with that season's sizes, joined the way NHC describes, starting from the forecast's first position. Inside the whole way means every six-hour best-track point from 12 to 120 hours lies in it. Timing is not checked, as NHC's wording is about the path. Nothing returned counts forecasts whose storm track ended or stopped being tropical within five days. Those are never folded into either side. Forecasts shorter than five days are left out: Atlantic 2,236, East Pacific 2,171. The cone here leaves out the 60 h circle, as NHC's graphic page does (NHC's cone page lists it). Starting the cone at the 12 h circle instead of the first position lowers the share by 1.6 points in the Atlantic and 2.8 in the East Pacific, in the referee's recount after the data, which decides nothing. The upper end of the Atlantic interval is 0.4 points above the lower end of NHC's range.

Forecasts of one storm share most of their track, so they are not independent. Each interval is a storm-level block bootstrap: storms are drawn again with replacement, all of a drawn storm's forecasts come with it, 2,000 times. Reading: consistent if the whole interval lies inside 60 to 70 percent, above if it lies above 70, below if it lies below 60, otherwise cannot tell.

A second ruler: the averages NHC publishes

NHC publishes, in its tables titled “Average NHC Atlantic Track Forecast Errors” and the same for the Eastern North Pacific, the average great-circle distance between its official forecast position and the best track for every season and lead time, with the number of cases. We recounted the same average from the same forecast files and the best track, using NHC's rule that both the forecast time and the verifying time must be tropical or subtropical. A row reads consistent if our average is within the larger of 1 n mi and 2 percent of NHC's, and cannot tell if the case counts differ by more than the larger of 2 cases and 5 percent, since then the samples differ. Atlantic: 112 rows, 111 consistent, 0 above, 0 below and 1 cannot tell, every cannot tell row a row whose case counts differ. East Pacific: 112 rows, 109 consistent, 0 above, 0 below and 3 cannot tell, every cannot tell row a row whose case counts differ.

What this page holds and what it leaves out

The forecasts are NHC's official ones as stored in its public forecast files, one per storm and six-hour initial time. In the East Pacific only forecasts starting east of 140 W are read. Central Pacific forecasts are left out. Set aside by the plan's frame, each forecast counted in the first reason that applies in the order the run tests them: 916 not a tropical cyclone at the initial time, 755 East Pacific forecast west of 140 W, 754 forecasts in a season without cone radii (second ruler only), 134 no best-track point at the initial time, 29 no best track for this storm. The circle sizes come from the NHC cone page and its copies in the Internet Archive, each season using the table that names it.

The cone's day one to three and day four to five shading, wind field size, watches and warnings, and intensity forecasts are not read. A forecast outside the cone is a forecast outside the cone. This page does not say why the storm went where it went.
