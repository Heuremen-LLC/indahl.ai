Division: HERMES

Large platforms describe their own conduct in more than one public record. A report or a library gives totals. A regulator’s database or a campaign finance file holds entries one by one. SOCIAL reads one public record against another that covers the same activity over the same period, and prints where the two agree as well as where they differ.

Each section below is one pair of records, with its own plan, rules, sources and data. Every row reads above, below, consistent or cannot tell, and every cannot tell carries one reason, STRADDLES_EDGE, TOO_WIDE_TO_RESOLVE or NO_INTERVAL_FORMABLE. A row where one side holds no figure reads cannot tell with the reason NO_INTERVAL_FORMABLE. NOTHING_RETURNED is the status of a failed read of a source and is not an outcome. A reading that uses a rule set after the data says so on its row or in its column. We measure gaps and name no cause.

Two filings, one platform

Each very large online platform in the EU states its content moderation counts twice. Its transparency report gives totals for each half-year in a template the European Commission fixed. Its statements of reasons go one by one into the Commission’s DSA Transparency Database. We read the report’s figures against the count of the same platform’s statements for the same half-year, and print where the two agree as well as where they differ. YouTube, Google Maps and Google Play report their ads moderation in separate files, so their main rows leave out statements flagged as ads, and their ads are read on rows of their own.

Two of the four checks committed with the plan failed. Under the plan as committed every row would read cannot tell. We located both failures before reading the comparisons and replaced the checks in dated addenda. The readings below use those addenda, and every rule set after the data is labelled on its row.

Database aggregates read 9 November 2026 (HST), with statements submitted up to 7 November 2026. Reports are read in the latest version each platform publishes.

How we read it

The stated figure is the one the platform printed in its report, quoted from the file with its name and date in the data. The delivered figure is the number of statements the same platform filed, counted from the Commission’s monthly aggregates of the database. We count statements by the date each decision applies from, including statements filed late up to the newest aggregate, and again by the date each statement was submitted. The band runs between the two counts. It also carries the partially automated category, statements that name only Iceland, Liechtenstein or Norway, and the measures the law does not require in the database: orders from authorities and spam. Above means the database holds more than the report states, beyond the whole band and by more than a tenth. Below means it holds less, by the same test. Either must clear its edge by at least 100 statements. Consistent means the whole band lies within a tenth of the stated figure. Cannot tell means none of these. Some differences neither source sizes, so no reading names a cause. A below reading can come from statements the law does not require where the recipient’s contact details are unknown or for deceptive high-volume commercial content that a report does not label as spam (DSA Article 17(2)), from a filing that counts one decision per item, per account or per restriction type, from a report that counts content filtered at upload, and from statements filed after the newest aggregate. An above reading can come from how each filing decides which decisions concern the EU, and from one decision filed as more than one statement, since the aggregates cannot detect re-filings.

The plan and its rules were committed before the first count of the database by platform. Changes made after the first counts are dated addenda to the plan. Rows that use a rule defined after the data say so. These cover the separate ads files, the notice rows, notes in which a platform says a field counts something the database does not hold, bounds taken from a report’s own restriction columns, splits the two filings label differently, and a minimum of 100 statements for an above or below reading. On six days in February and May 2026 the Commission’s simple aggregate counts statements twice. We read the complete aggregate, which matches the Commission’s published daily totals within 0.05% on every day but three at the turn of September and October 2026. The bands carry those three days, and two days in November 2026 with no published file. That allowance is wide on purpose, and it is the reason many trusted flagger and own-initiative illegal-content rows read cannot tell.

How we read it

The two filings count different things, so a gap is a measured difference between two filings and we name no cause for it. Google’s library holds only ads that Google classes as election ads, and it counts them for the advertiser whoever paid. A committee that buys through an agency reports a payment to the agency, not to Google, so its direct payments to Google can only fall short. Those rows read Cannot tell unless the committee’s payments to others for ads, digital or media cannot close the gap either. Card payments, reimbursements and consulting fees are not counted in that pool. Ads paid from accounts that do not report to the FEC appear only on Google’s side. Ads that Google does not class as election ads, ads that run under another advertiser’s account and refunds a committee receives from Google appear only on the FEC side, so a committee’s payments can exceed Google’s figure. Other Google products, such as email and office software, appear only on the FEC side and count only in the all-rows figure. Committee names are as each cycle’s committee file holds them on the day we read it, and some committees have changed names since their ads ran. How we read it

The report side is TikTok's own figure, summed over the EU markets it lists. Its range runs from that sum to the sum plus what the unlisted EU markets, Norway, Iceland and Liechtenstein could hold under TikTok's own listing rule. We do not know whether TikTok files statements for users in Norway, Iceland or Liechtenstein, so the range allows both. Rates in the report are printed to three decimals, and the range carries that rounding.

The database side counts TikTok statements with video content and a removal decision, not ads, dated by the day the decision was applied. Its range runs across that day shifted one day either way, and across a narrow and a wide reading of video. The narrow reading keeps removals for breaking TikTok's rules only. The wide reading adds images, synthetic media and other content, because TikTok counts photo posts and stories as videos.

No range on this page is a statistical interval. Both sides are full counts, and each range shows how far a figure moves across the matching choices we fixed before reading the data. The gap range is the database range minus the report range, as a share of the report figure for counts and in percentage points for shares. Above means the database figure is higher than the report allows under every choice. Below means it is lower under every choice. Consistent means the gap range includes zero and stays within 5% for counts or 1 percentage point for shares. Cannot tell means the gap range includes zero and reaches past that size, or a check on the files failed. A row where one side holds no figure reads Cannot tell with the reason NO_INTERVAL_FORMABLE. Every Cannot tell carries one reason beside the word, and a failed read of a source is a status, NOTHING_RETURNED, not an outcome. We measure gaps and name no cause.

Removed within 24 hours is compared with the days between the posting date and the decision date. The database keeps both as whole days, so its side is a range.

Some limits we cannot size. TikTok's report counts videos removed from fake accounts and LIVE sessions apart from videos removed, and gives no market split for either. The database could hold statements for them, and one decision filed twice counts twice. We cannot size these, so an Above may include them. Restored videos are not among them, because TikTok counts them in both its removal and its restored figures.

Our plan first let the submission date set one end of the database range. Before we set any figure against another, the files showed that TikTok filed no statement on some days and, from July 2025 to January 2026, filed up to 97% of a month's removal statements one to three months after the decision. TikTok's report counts cases closed in the quarter, so we date by the decision and print the count by submission date beside it. The data file keeps the outcome under the first rule for every row.

Our committed check on the database files failed on 11 February 2026 and 30 September to 2 October 2026, so Oct to Dec 2025, Jan to Mar 2026 and Apr to Jun 2026 read Cannot tell. The column marked defined after the data replaces that check with TikTok's own daily totals, published on the database's download page. They match the files exactly on every day both cover except 46. On 41 of the matching days neither side holds a statement. Where the files hold more statements than TikTok's total, the excess can come off the low end of the database range (the largest is 8 May 2026, by 858,890). Where they hold fewer, the shortfall is added to the high end (the largest is 30 September 2026, by 184,124).
