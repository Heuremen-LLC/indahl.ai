The Hoverboard Table

Sound can hold things up

Sound is air being squeezed and stretched, very fast. Bounce a sound off a wall and the sound going up and the sound coming back stack on top of each other. That’s a standing wave.

Some spots in a standing wave stay perfectly still. They’re called nodes. Push a tiny, light bead near one and the sound shoves it into the node, and it floats there.

Red is squeezed air, blue is stretched air. We drew it slowed way down, and as if the gap were tuned just right.

Build one

Scientists at the University of Bristol designed a levitator anyone can build: TinyLev. It has 72 little speakers at 40 kHz, too high for you to hear, in a 3D-printed frame, and it floats foam beads and drops of water. It runs on about 10 watts. Build it with a grown-up.

The hoverboard table

In June we designed a table 6 feet by 6 feet, tiled in triangles, with a 40 kHz speaker at every corner: about 1,200 speakers and $1,510 in parts. The idea: a skateboard that glides like it’s on ice.

What held up. At 40 kHz the wavelength is 8.6 mm, and the triangle grid math works.

What didn’t. We said the push grows with the square of the number of speakers. Across a whole table it only grows in a straight line. We said each node pushes about 3 grams, and we never checked that number. And we put a plastic sheet on top, which would bounce the sound straight back down.

The real road. A metal plate shaking at ultrasound can float a flat object on a cushion of air thinner than a hair (Hashimoto, Koike and Ueha, 1996). That cushion is real. But it only works for very flat, very smooth things, so a skateboard with you on it is not happening yet.

Your ankles are safe. For now.
