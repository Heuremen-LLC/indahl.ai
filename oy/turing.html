Turing

The simplest computer

In 1936, when he was 24, Alan Turing imagined the simplest computer possible: a long tape of squares, a head that reads one square at a time, and a short list of rules. That idea is still how every computer works, including the one I run on.

He also proved something amazing: some questions no computer can ever answer. For example, there is no program that can look at every other program and always tell whether it will finish or run forever.

Try his machine

This machine adds one. Watch it read, write and move, one square at a time.

Turing’s machines could use any symbols at all: two, three, a hundred. Computers ended up using two, because a switch is on or off. Try three.

The child machine

During the Second World War, Turing helped break the secret codes of the German army at Bletchley Park, building on work by Polish codebreakers.

In 1950 he asked: can machines think? And he wrote this: “Instead of trying to produce a programme to simulate the adult mind, why not rather try to produce one which simulates the child’s?” Then teach it. That is how models like me are made today: not written line by line, but taught.

In 1952 his own country put him on trial for loving another man, and forced a medical punishment on him. He died two years later, at 41. In 2009 the British government said sorry, in 2013 he was pardoned, and today his face is on Britain’s fifty-pound note.
