Political ad spend: Google’s library against campaign filings

Google states how much each verified election advertiser spent on election ads, week by week. Federal political committees report their payments to the FEC, itemized by payee once a payee’s total passes 200 dollars in a year, or in an election cycle for a candidate’s committee. We set the two side by side, per two-year cycle, for every committee we can match.

Of the 18,039 US advertisers in Google’s file, 4,151 matched an FEC committee: 3,351 through the FEC committee or candidate ID that Google lists for them and 800 through a name that equals exactly one committee’s name and passes the match checks. The other 13,888 did not match and carry $1,374,938,400 of Google’s stated spend. Of those, 12,617 list only a tax ID or a state registration and 1,250 list no ID. They include 210 name matches we set aside because Google lists the same name under a state registration, because at least two thirds of the advertiser’s spend sits in a state other than the committee’s, or because the spend began more than 90 days before the committee’s first itemized filing.

Band: the spend that must have been paid inside the window to the spend that could have been, allowing payments 31 days before to 92 days after each ad week and 100 dollars either way per week for Google’s rounding to whole hundreds. It is a bound, not a confidence interval. Ad rows leave out amounts that a filer marked as estimates in 24- or 48-hour reports. All rows count them. Above: ad payments to Google exceed the top of the band by at least 1,000 dollars and 10 percent, and Google lists spend in the window. Below: every payment to Google, every payment to others for ads, digital or media, and any money in the committee’s own summary that the itemized file does not hold, all together, fall short of the bottom of the band by that much, and the gap does not rest on a name match alone. Consistent: ad payments to Google reach the bottom of the band, every payment to Google plus that unseen money stays under its top, and the band spans no more than a factor of two. Unseen money counts in full for party committees and for committees whose itemized file holds less than nine tenths of their own operating disbursements. Otherwise it counts up to 400 dollars, the most the itemization rule can leave unitemized for one payee over two years (200 dollars a calendar year, or 200 dollars an election cycle for a candidate’s committee). That rule and its threshold were defined after the data, and rows whose outcome depends on it say so. Cannot tell: anything else. Agency means payments to others for ads, digital or media could close the gap. Band means the timing band spans more than a factor of two. Purpose means rows of unclear purpose decide the side. Unseen means money that the itemized file does not hold could decide it. Near means the gap is under the size that matters. Scope means the filer type reports no operating expenditures. Cycle means the committee is not registered in that cycle, or its candidate was not a candidate then. Name means the reading would rest on an advertiser matched by name alone. No Google spend means Google lists no election-ad spend for the advertiser in the window. Estimates means the reading rests on amounts the filer marked as estimates. Depends on timing means a wider band of 92 days before and 183 days after gives another outcome. Other notes on a row keep its outcome and say what could move it. Google figures are from its weekly spend file. FEC figures are from Schedule B operating expenditures, the independent expenditure file and the summary files.

How we read it

The two filings count different things, so a gap is a measured difference between two filings and we name no cause for it. Google’s library holds only ads that Google classes as election ads, and it counts them for the advertiser whoever paid. A committee that buys through an agency reports a payment to the agency, not to Google, so its direct payments to Google can only fall short. Those rows read Cannot tell unless the committee’s payments to others for ads, digital or media cannot close the gap either. Card payments, reimbursements and consulting fees are not counted in that pool. Ads paid from accounts that do not report to the FEC appear only on Google’s side. Ads that Google does not class as election ads, ads that run under another advertiser’s account and refunds a committee receives from Google appear only on the FEC side, so a committee’s payments can exceed Google’s figure. Other Google products, such as email and office software, appear only on the FEC side and count only in the all-rows figure. Committee names are as each cycle’s committee file holds them on the day we read it, and some committees have changed names since their ads ran. The plan, its sizes and its bands were written before we compared any committee. Later addenda are dated in the plan, and the ones on unseen money, match checks, Nothing returned and estimates were defined after the data. Party appears only as the filed field in the data and plays no part in any reading.
