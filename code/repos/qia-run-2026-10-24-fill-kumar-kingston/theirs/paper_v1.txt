Separability does not certify zero-noise extrapolation:
Factorial diagnostics in dynamic quantum circuits
Aryan Kumar1
1Amity International School, Sector-46, Gurugram, India
Separately amplifying gate, latency, and mid-circuit measurement errors can simplify zero-noise
extrapolation, but a weak interaction between amplification knobs does not establish an accurate
zero-noise limit. We distinguish observable separability, response-model adequacy, and calibration of
that limit, and test them in a pre-specified 23 factorial experiment on three IBM dynamic circuits.
For the primary echoed spectator observable, the latency–measurement coefficient is bLM = +0.0007
with a nominal 95% bootstrap interval [−0.0033, +0.0049]. A separately planned follow-up meets a
±0.005 equivalence criterion and recovers two injected interactions; both repeat runs nevertheless
trigger the drift rule. Multi-knob extrapolations have smaller median absolute errors than the
single-knob comparisons, yet all five original strategies exclude the known ideal value for three of six
observables. Duration controls support a model-dependent contribution to the spectator deficit from
unequal decay rates in native padding and added delay. We give an explicit construction showing
why measurements at two measurement-knob levels cannot identify the extrapolated limit without
response assumptions. A post-hoc precision analysis and an additive full-factorial estimator expose
a further bias–variance trade-off hidden by point-error comparisons. The experiment provides a
diagnostic of the implemented response surface, not a certification of physical channel independence
or mitigation accuracy.
I.
INTRODUCTION
Zero-noise extrapolation (ZNE) estimates an ideal quan-
tum observable from deliberately noisier circuit execu-
tions [1, 2]. Its validity depends on the response to noise
amplification and on whether the extrapolated coordi-
nate corresponds to the intended noise-free circuit. Noise
can be increased by stretching control pulses [2] or, more
conveniently, by “folding” gates: a gate U is replaced by
UU †U, which ideally does the same thing but roughly
triples the gate noise [3, 4].
When a circuit contains several kinds of noise, a natural
generalization is to amplify each kind separately and fit
a function of several noise parameters [5, 6]. Whether
the extra work pays off is debated: a published Comment
argues that conventional extrapolation with a single noise
parameter already works when the noise is not identically
distributed [7].
Time-correlated noise adds a further
complication: it is hard to amplify such noise without
also changing its spectrum, especially when individual
gates are amplified, although global folding can remain
reliable [8].
Dynamic circuits raise this question in a sharp form.
In a dynamic circuit some qubits are measured partway
through, and later operations are chosen according to
the outcomes, which classical hardware computes in real
time [9, 10]. Such circuits suffer, among other errors, from
three physically distinct kinds of noise: errors in the gates,
decoherence of every qubit while it waits for the classical
decision (the feedforward latency), and errors caused by
the mid-circuit measurements (MCMs) themselves. We
will call these G, L and M. Folding the gates reaches
only the first.
Several groups have begun to amplify the other two.
The idea of adding idle time, in the form of identity or
delay pulses, to increase the noise before extrapolating
goes back at least to Tomkins and de Sousa [11]. Carrera
Vazquez et al. stretched the latency of conditional opera-
tions with delays inside a staggered dynamical-decoupling
sequence and extrapolated linearly to zero delay; without
the decoupling, they observed oscillations that prevented
an accurate extrapolation [9]. Santos and Uzdin ampli-
fied measurement errors by repeating each measurement
and acting on the parity of the outcomes [12]. Bar, San-
tos and Uzdin extended layered gate mitigation to dy-
namic circuits while leaving the mid-circuit measurements
unscaled [13], and Uzdin has proposed a virtual noise-
scaling method described as compatible with dynamic
circuits [14]. Shirgure and Niu pointed out that globally
folding the dynamic parts of a circuit scales all three
kinds of noise together, with a single scale factor [15].
And Farrell et al., running a 139-qubit dissipative pro-
tocol with mid-circuit resets on ibm boston, used ZNE
that amplified only two-qubit gates and idling, and found
that it did not effectively mitigate the increased noise
sensitivity of their circuits with fewer environment qubits;
they identify imperfect mid-circuit resets as a secondary
source of noise [16].
Other work characterizes or cancels dynamic-circuit
noise instead of extrapolating it, through probabilistic
error cancellation [17], readout mitigation for mid-circuit
measurements [18], learning and characterization of the
noise of measurements and feedforward [19–21], error-
aware compilation [22] and learned dynamical decou-
pling [23]. In a study of decoupling, Rahman, Egger and
Arenz used repeated mid-circuit measurements to amplify
measurement-induced noise on neighbouring qubits [24].
Suppose, then, that we can amplify G, L and M sep-
arately. Must we amplify them jointly and fit a model
with many parameters, or may we amplify one at a time
and simply combine the results? The answer depends on
whether the three kinds of noise are separable: whether

each one reduces an observable by a factor that does not
depend on the others. If they are, the effects multiply, the
logarithm of the observable is a sum of separate terms,
and separate one-dimensional extrapolations can be com-
bined, provided each extrapolation is itself valid. If they
are not — if, for example, the tones used to measure one
qubit also dephase an idle neighbour while it waits for
the feedforward — then the effects do not multiply, and
omitting the resulting interactions can bias the combined
extrapolation. Separability in this sense is a property of
the observable and the knobs, not of the physical channels:
channels that act quite independently need not combine
multiplicatively, as Sec. II E will show. Our focus is a di-
rect factorial diagnostic of this assumption for separately
controlled gate, latency, and measurement operations in
small dynamic circuits; we do not claim that multivariate
extrapolation or factorial interaction analysis is new.
We separate three questions that are often conflated:
whether the observable is separable over the settings mea-
sured, whether the extrapolation model remains valid
outside those settings, and whether its zero coordinate
removes the relevant noise. We do not propose a new
way of amplifying noise; our three amplification “knobs”
are adapted from the work cited above. Instead we ask,
with an experiment designed for the purpose, whether
the noise they amplify is separable on real hardware. The
experiment is a factorial design: each knob is set low or
high, in all eight combinations, on three small dynamic
circuits whose ideal outputs are known exactly, and the
analysis was fixed in advance.
Along the way we de-
rive simple formulas for the bias of one-at-a-time and of
uniform extrapolation in terms of the interactions, and
we point out that coherent phase errors can masquerade
as interactions — a pitfall that our own first trial run
illustrates.
In brief, we find that once the idle periods are echoed,
the interaction we considered most likely is consistent
with zero at the resolution of the experiment; two later
runs, under different calibrations, also found it small, and
the analysis recovered interactions of known size that we
injected on the hardware. Simple one-at-a-time or uni-
form extrapolation does about as well as a full joint fit,
and in two of our three circuits, amplifying every kind of
noise matters far more than how the amplifications are
combined. But separability is not accuracy: for half of
our observables, all five original extrapolation strategies
exclude the ideal value. Duration controls support a con-
tribution from the latency knob under an explicit slot-cost
model. An additional post-hoc analysis gives a construc-
tive identifiability limit, exact estimator weights, and an
additive fit using the complete cube. These additions
sharpen the interpretation of the existing experiment;
they are not new confirmatory hardware tests.
We begin in Sec. II with a simple model of how the
three kinds of noise might combine, and work out what
goes wrong when they do not combine multiplicatively.
Section III describes how each kind of noise is amplified,
the circuits, and the plan of the experiment. Section IV
reports what we found: first in trial runs that test the
knobs, then in the main experiment, with a positive con-
trol, in a comparison of extrapolation strategies and in
two repeat runs, and finally in two checks, made after the
main runs, on our error bars and on the latency knob.
Section V discusses what the results do and do not show,
and Sec. VI contains concluding comments.

A.
A simple model
Consider first a circuit with only one kind of noise,
amplified by a factor λ, where λ = 1 is the native circuit.
If each unit of noise multiplies the expectation value
E of an observable by the same factor, then E(λ) =
E(0) e−γλ, and the extrapolated value E(0) follows from
a straight-line fit of ln E against λ. A sufficient condition
is that the measured observable is an eigenoperator of
the adjoint noise channel, with a positive eigenvalue, and
amplification repeats that channel without changing the
ideal operation. A single Markovian decay mode also has
this form. An arbitrary observable under a Pauli channel,
or a sum of decay modes, need not follow one exponential.

III.
THE EXPERIMENT
A.
Turning up one kind of noise at a time
How can each kind of noise be amplified without dis-
turbing the others? Our knobs manage this only approx-
imately, and throughout, λ denotes a knob’s nominal
setting: how many times a CZ is applied, how far the
window is stretched, how many times a measurement is
repeated. That the noise grows in proportion to it is an
assumption, which Sec. IV A shows to fail for the measure-
ment knob. For the gates, the standard method is folding.
We replace each CZ gate by CZλG, an odd number of
repetitions that ideally acts as a single CZ but multiplies
the gate noise roughly λG-fold, with every CZ randomly
Pauli-twirled and with barriers that stop the compiler
from cancelling the repetitions. Folding also lengthens
the time before the measurement, during which the other
qubits wait, so the gate knob amplifies some idle noise as
well; we did not compensate for this.
For the latency, we lengthen the window during which
the qubits wait for the feedforward. Following Carrera
Vazquez et al. [9], who stretch a duration τ by a factor
c = (τ + δ)/τ and extrapolate to c = 0, we add free
evolution of length (λL−1)τw, so that its nominal duration
coordinate is λLτw. Identifying λL = 0 with a physical
zero-length window requires the assumed baseline timing
and scaling model. Here τw = 3tslot + τff, where tslot =
tmeas + 2tX is the length of one measurement slot and τff
is the feedforward latency. Because τff is not published
for each device, we assume τff = 1.0 µs. This choice
changes what λL = 0 means in physical time, but not
the interaction estimates, which compare only the two
levels that were actually run; Sec. IV C shows how much
it matters for the extrapolations. More generally, the
interaction estimates rest only on the levels run, whereas
every extrapolation to λL = 0 also assumes that the
window as run decoheres at the same rate as the free
evolution added to stretch it (Sec. V).
A first trial run, described in Sec. IV A, showed that an
unprotected window lets an idle qubit rotate coherently,
which by Eq. (11) is exactly what we must avoid. Every
window therefore carries a fixed staggered echo [Fig. 1(b)],
similar in spirit to the staggered decoupling sequence of
Ref. [9]: qubits at even positions along the chain receive X
pulses at d/2 and d, and those at odd positions at d/4 and
3d/4, where d is the total free evolution. For ideal pulses,
this refocuses static Z rotations on every qubit and static
ZZ coupling between neighbours. Each qubit receives two
pulses in each explicit delay sequence at every setting, so
their count does not scale with λL. The knob changes the
programmed free-evolution duration; finite pulse widths
and residual coherent or time-dependent errors need not
obey a pure dephasing-and-relaxation model.
For the measurements, we follow Santos and Uzdin [12]:
each mid-circuit measurement that feeds a decision is
repeated λM times, and the correction is conditioned
on the parity of the outcomes.
Here there is a trap.
Repeating a measurement takes time, so turning up λM
would also lengthen the window, and measurement noise
would secretly carry latency noise with it. To prevent
this, we pad every λM = 1 window with 2tslot of echoed
idling. This matches the nominal slot-duration budget
at each latency setting, making the measurement knob
compare measurement operations with padded waiting.
It is not a measurement of the backend’s realized schedule.
Conditional control latency, twirl-dependent pulses, and
compiler scheduling can leave timing differences, especially
in circuits with several measurements and conditions. The
records validate inserted delays and instruction counts,
not the full executed pulse timeline; all separability claims
therefore concern the implemented knobs.
All twirling was applied by hand and recorded, because
IBM’s built-in measurement twirling does not cover mea-
surements that feed conditions. Mid-circuit measurements
use the dedicated instruction measure 2, and terminal
measurements the standard measure.
B.
Three circuits with known answers
Mitigation can be judged only against a known answer,
so we chose three small dynamic circuits whose ideal
expectation values are all exactly +1. The primary one,
the spectator probe of Fig. 1(a), is built to expose the
interaction we considered most likely. Three qubits sit in
a row: a target t, an ancilla a and a spectator s, with a
and s prepared in |+⟩. A CZ gate between a and t, ideally
the identity because t starts in |0⟩, exposes gate noise.
The ancilla is then measured mid-circuit, the window
runs, and an X correction is applied to t according to
the outcome. The spectator’s only job is to record how
much it dephases while its neighbour is measured and the
feedforward is computed. We measure ⟨Xs⟩, the primary
observable, and ⟨ZtZa⟩, which tests the feedforward itself.

C.
Design
Each knob takes the values 1 and 3, and all eight
combinations, the corners of a cube, are run for each
circuit. Two centre points, (2, 2, 1) and (2, 2, 3), test for
curvature. Because λM must be odd (for even λM the
parity of identical outcomes would always be zero), it has
no centre level, so the centre point is repeated at both
of its levels. The value λG = 2 is realized by folding
all the CZs of a twirled instance of the circuit, or none
of them, each with probability 1/2. This is a mixture
of endpoint circuits, not a physical intermediate gate-
noise level. It is linear in E, not in ln E, and therefore
cannot identify gate-axis curvature. To first order in the
gate contrast and under the quadratic response model, the
centre contrast measures c ≃−kL, giving the approximate
shift 3c −3kG −3kM. Mixture imbalance and the log-
of-mixture correction are quantified in Appendix A; a
measured centre contrast should not be read as a model-
free estimate of latency curvature. A comparison set adds
the latency-only settings (1, 2, 1), (1, 4, 1) and (1, 5, 1) and
the gate-only setting (2, 1, 1). Each setting of the main
experiment used 2 × 104 shots, divided among 20 random
twirls; the trial runs used 5 × 103 shots per setting, on
the probe alone.
How small an interaction could this detect? An ob-
servable with values ±1, estimated from N shots, has a
standard error of at most 1/
√
N. A coded coefficient such
as bLM is half the difference between the mean values on
two halves of the cube, so at N = 2 × 104 its standard
error is at most 0.0025, or 0.0025/Emin on the logarithmic
scale, where Emin is the smallest of the eight values. The
trial run on the main device, scaled to the same number
of shots, gave a standard error of 0.0016 for bLM. From
this we computed, before the main experiment, that an
interaction of 0.0044 would be detected with 80% proba-
bility in a two-sided test at the 5% level (0.0052 at the
most stringent Holm level); we call 0.0044 the minimum
detectable effect.
D.
Pre-specified analysis
The analysis was specified before collecting the confir-
matory data; the trial runs informed the design. The plan
named one primary hypothesis, H1: that bLM is non-zero
for the probe spectator, judged by a bootstrap 95% confi-
dence interval together with a Holm correction over the
four interaction terms. Three secondary hypotheses asked
whether bLM exceeds the other pairwise interactions (H2),
whether the extra bias of uniform over joint extrapolation
grows with the size of the interactions across the six ob-
servables (H3, using independent halves of the data), and
whether bLM is stable across days (H4). An exploratory
comparison (E1) asked how much the echo reduces the
apparent interaction. The plan also fixed the logarithmic
scale as primary; bootstrap intervals with at least 2000
replicates, resampling twirls and shots; a drift rule that
sets aside, for separate analysis, any later run in which
the reported readout or CZ error of a qubit in use changes
by more than a factor of two, its T1 or T2 falls by more
than half, or the value at the native setting (1, 1, 1) shifts
by more than five standard errors; and an automatic
trial-run validation on the trial runs.
E.
Hardware and follow-up design
The experiment ran on IBM Quantum processors
through Qiskit Runtime (Qiskit 2.5.2, qiskit-ibm-runtime
0.50.0). The first two trial runs used ibm kingston. The
main experiment used ibm marrakesh, preceded by an
echoed trial run with the same validation criteria except
for the measurement-knob tests, whose circuits were not
repeated. On ibm marrakesh the circuits used physical
qubits 4–11 in chain order: 4–6 for the probe, 4–9 for tele-
portation and 4–11 for the GHZ state. Each circuit was
compiled onto this fixed chain, and an automatic check
confirmed that compilation added no two-qubit gates and
kept every measurement, conditional operation and delay.
After analysing these runs, we planned a follow-up on
the probe with three aims: to repeat the primary test

7
days later, to inject interactions of known size, and to
measure the cost of the padding at two settings of the
latency knob. In one batch, in random order, it ran the
eight corners as in the main experiment; the same corners
with two doses of an injected interaction (Sec. IV B), at
104 shots per setting; and the settings (1, 1, 1) and (1, 3, 1)
without padding, at 2 × 104. The follow-up analysis was
specified before data collection, and the experiment used
the same qubits more than four days after the main run.
Calibration details are given in Appendix B.
F.
A check in simulation
Before the hardware experiment, we ran the analysis
on simulated data (Qiskit Aer) from a noise model with
known coefficients; the numbers in this subsection are
therefore simulated. With separable noise the analysis
found bLM = −0.0008 [−0.0048, +0.0032], against a true
value of zero, and raised no false alarm in 20 repeti-
tions. With a synthetic latency × measurement interac-
tion added, it found −0.0581 [−0.0633, −0.0533], against
the true −0.0562, and detected the interaction in all
20 repetitions; the simulated estimators also reproduced
Eqs. (3) and (4) to four decimal places. The simulation
also shows how an interaction misleads uniform scaling:
with the synthetic interaction, uniform extrapolation over-
shot the ideal value (1.0745, against 0.9014 for the joint
fit), as Eq. (4) predicts for a negative bLM.
IV.
WHAT WE FOUND
The empirical results in this section are from IBM
Quantum hardware; Sec. IV G presents a post-hoc reanal-
ysis and an analytic variance comparison. Uncertainties
are 95% bootstrap confidence intervals; the hypothesis
tests and the figures use separate bootstrap runs on the
same data, so a few bounds differ in the last digit. For
the follow-up, as its plan specified, they come from the
instance-level bootstrap of Sec. IV E.
A.
Do the knobs do what we intend?
The trial runs taught us two lessons. The first concerns
the measurement knob. If each repetition of a measure-
ment misreported the outcome independently with prob-
ability p, the parity of λM repetitions would be wrong
with probability [1 −(1 −2p)λM ]/2, nearly three times
as often for λM = 3 as for a single measurement. To
check this, the first trial run, on ibm kingston, included
circuits that prepare the ancilla in |0⟩or |1⟩and measure
it once or three times. A single measurement erred with
probability 0.0209, but the parity of three repetitions was
wrong only 0.0309 of the time, far less than the 0.0601
that independence predicts, a difference of 6.8 standard
errors [Fig. 2(a)]. Moreover, each successive repetition
disagreed with the prepared state more often than the last
(0.0192, 0.0253 and 0.0306). These observations reject
the identical independent bit-flip model; correlated errors,
repetition-dependent error rates, or measurement-induced
state changes can contribute, consistent with the back-
action studied in Ref. [20]. The measurement knob thus
amplifies the effect of the measurement process as a whole,
not simply readout error, and, as our plan specified, we
interpret interactions involving M accordingly.
The second lesson concerns the latency knob. The first
trial run deliberately had no echo, so that stretching the
window would add nothing but idle time, and it showed
what Carrera Vazquez et al. had warned of [9]. As the win-
dow was stretched five-fold, the spectator’s ⟨Xs⟩fell from
0.97 to 0.29, but not exponentially, as Eq. (1) assumes; it
followed a cosine almost perfectly [Fig. 2(b); χ2 = 0.6 for
a cosine with 2 degrees of freedom, against 349.5 for an ex-
ponential with 3]. The spectator was rotating coherently,
by about 0.27 rad per step of λL, presumably because of
a static frequency offset or ZZ coupling of the kind the
echo is designed to cancel. A straight-line extrapolation
of this curve to zero latency gives the impossible value
1.20. And, as Eq. (11) warns, the run also showed an
apparent interaction, bLM = +0.0198 [+0.0113, +0.0283],
which, to leading order, Eq. (11) alone would explain
with aM ≈−0.07 rad, given aL ≈0.27 rad (we did not
measure aM independently).
The echo suppressed both problems. On the same de-
vice and qubits, ⟨Xs⟩now decayed slowly and smoothly,
from 0.97 to 0.90 over the same range; an exponential
fitted well (χ2 = 4.0 for 3 degrees of freedom; a co-
sine fits about as well, χ2 = 2.5 for 2, but only with
a rotation five times slower than before, 0.054 rad per
step); and the interaction became consistent with zero,
bLM = −0.0014 [−0.0055, +0.0026]. When the main ex-
periment moved to ibm marrakesh, a repeat of the echoed
trial run there showed no rotation either (bLM = +0.0029
[−0.0032, +0.0090]), although the decay flattened at the
longest window and the exponential fit was less good
(χ2 = 19.6 for 3 degrees of freedom). (These trial-run
intervals come from the 500-replicate bootstrap of the
trial-run validation.)
The trial runs also confirmed that duration matching
was necessary. On ibm marrakesh, padding a λM = 1
window with 2tslot of echoed idling lowered ⟨Xs⟩by 0.064,
ten times its standard error; without the padding, the
measurement knob would have carried this much latency
noise with it. (Section IV F returns to this number.)

Would the analysis have seen an interaction if there
had been one? The minimum detectable effect answers
this only on paper, so in the follow-up we injected in-
teractions of known size. Just before the spectator is
read out, we apply a virtual rotation about the z axis,
which IBM implements as a change of the reference frame
for later pulses: by θ = a(xL + xM) in half of the in-
stances and by −θ in the other half.
For a common
pre-rotation state averaged equally over the two signs,
this multiplies ⟨Xs⟩by exactly cos θ, whatever phase that
state carries: it is the mechanism of Sec. II E, made ex-
act in expectation. In finite samples, random twirls and
temporal state variation can prevent exact cancellation
of the sine term. It shifts b0 and bLM by 1
2 ln cos 2a and
leaves the other six coefficients unchanged. We chose two
doses, a = 0.0997 and 0.1405 rad, which shift bLM by
−0.010 and −0.020, two and four times the bound above.
The measured shifts were −0.0104 [−0.0148, −0.0058] and
−0.0213 [−0.0253, −0.0172], that is, 1.04 [0.58, 1.48] and
1.06 [0.86, 1.26] times the injected sizes, so the criterion
fixed in the plan, that each interval exclude zero and
contain the prediction, was met. At the corners where
the rotation acted, ln⟨Xs⟩fell by 0.022 and 0.016 for the
smaller dose and by 0.038 and 0.040 for the larger, against
0.020 and 0.040 predicted, and none of the other six coef-
ficients moved significantly, or by as much as 0.005. The
analysis thus recovers interactions of this size, at the size
injected.
One result of this run was unexpected. The feedfor-
ward observable ⟨ZtZa⟩, which the rotation does not
touch, should not have changed at all, yet its bLM
was larger in the two rotated blocks than in the unro-
tated one, by +0.0056 [−0.0010, +0.0124] and +0.0080
[+0.0019, +0.0144]. In a check made after the run, ran-
domly exchanging instances between the blocks produced
a difference as large as that of the two rotated blocks
together, +0.0068, only 2% of the time. The rotation
can hardly be the cause. It changes only the reference
frame of another qubit, after the last feedforward opera-
tion, and three quarters of the difference comes from the
corners with (λL, λM) = (3, 1), where θ = 0, so that the
rotated circuits there differ from the unrotated ones only
in their random twirls; at those corners the spectator, too,
differs between blocks, by +0.0105 [−0.0002, +0.0204]
for the larger dose. Nor is it a steady drift, although
ln⟨ZtZa⟩did rise by about 0.02 over the two minutes of
the run; removing that trend leaves the difference intact.
The exploratory permutation p value is conditional on
exchangeability of the instances and is not the probability
that the discrepancy arose by chance. Because several
post-hoc diagnostics were examined, it is a warning about

9
reproducibility rather than a separately confirmed physi-
cal effect. Differences of this size between independently
built circuit sets call for caution.
The echo appears to have made the difference. With-
out it, in the first trial run, |bLM| was 0.0198; with it,
in the main experiment, 0.0007, a reduction of 0.0191
[0.0092, 0.0272]. Because these two runs also differ in
device, calibration and number of shots, the cleaner com-
parison is between the two trial runs on the same device,
in which |bLM| fell from 0.0198 to 0.0014.
Figure 4 puts this result in context by showing every
coefficient of Eq. (2) but the constant b0, for all six observ-
ables. The latency effect is the largest main effect for every
observable, and it exceeds every interaction, by more than
a factor of ten for all but the poorly determined GHZ
spectator. Of the 24 interaction coefficients, two have
intervals that exclude zero without correction: bGM for
the teleportation spectator (−0.0068 [−0.0126, −0.0009])
and bLM for the probe’s feedforward observable (−0.0056
[−0.0097, −0.0012]). About 1.2 such intervals would be
expected by chance if all 24 coefficients were zero, and
the two do not form a pattern; the second did not recur
consistently in later runs (Sec. IV D). Because the gate
knob also adds idle time, interactions involving G mix the
effects of gates and of idling; the latency × measurement
interaction, our primary target, does not suffer from this.
The GHZ intervals are too wide (up to about ±0.05) to
say much at the scale of the primary test.
One main effect deserves comment, because its sign is
surprising. For the probe, turning up the measurement
knob raises both observables (bM = +0.0326 for ⟨Xs⟩
and +0.0186 for ⟨ZtZa⟩). Because of duration matching,
however, the knob compares two extra measurements
with an equal time of echoed idling, so a positive bM
means only that, on this device, the idling did more harm
than the measurements; the duration control had already
shown that the padding alone lowers ⟨Xs⟩by 0.064. For
the teleportation observables and the GHZ parity, bM is
negative, and for the GHZ spectator it is consistent with
zero.
Finally, the centre points show that Eq. (1) is not ex-
actly linear in λL for every observable. For the probe spec-
tator the curvature excludes zero at both centre points,
with nearly equal values (−0.0144 [−0.0282, −0.0004]
and −0.0143 [−0.0261, −0.0030]); for the teleportation
spectator it excludes zero at one of them (+0.0188
[+0.0055, +0.0329]) and has the same sign at the other

[−0.1145, −0.0000]), with the opposite sign at the other
centre point. We return to the consequences in Secs. IV F
and V.
C.
Which extrapolation should one use?
With the interactions known, we can ask the practical
question: how well do the different strategies recover the
ideal value? Figure 5 shows the five estimates for each
observable.
The original multi-knob strategies gave similar median
point errors over these six observables. This descriptive
comparison does not establish statistical equivalence or
equal-budget performance. Their median absolute biases
over the six observables were 0.0502 for the joint fit, 0.0625
for one-at-a-time and 0.0525 for uniform scaling. These
errors are compatible with small interactions in several
observables, but the medians conceal observable-specific
differences and uncertainty. The two strategies that am-
plify only one kind of noise did worse, with median biases
of 0.1366 (latency-only) and 0.1828 (gate-only), 2.6 to 3.6
times those of the joint and uniform estimates, and their
intervals never contained the ideal value. (Our analysis
also fitted both exponentially; latency-only extrapolation
then has a median bias of 0.102, about twice that of the
joint and uniform estimates, and gate-only extrapolation
is almost unchanged.) For the teleportation and GHZ cir-
cuits, then, what matters most is not how the components
are combined, but that all of them are amplified.
The probe is the exception. Its measured gate-knob
effect is small, and here the multi-knob strategies gain
nothing from the measurement knob: because turning
that knob up raised the probe’s observables, extrapolating
it to zero lowers the estimate, by 0.036 on the logarith-
mic scale for the one-at-a-time ⟨Xs⟩, about half of that
estimate’s shortfall. For both probe observables, latency-
only extrapolation did about as well as the multi-knob
strategies.
Even the best strategies are not accurate everywhere,
however. Their intervals include the ideal value for both
teleportation observables, but for the probe spectator,
the probe’s feedforward observable and the GHZ parity,
the 95% intervals of every strategy lie below 1, and the
multi-knob strategies miss by 0.04 to 0.25. (For the GHZ
spectator, the joint and one-at-a-time intervals are too
wide to judge.) With only six observables, the test of
whether the extra bias of uniform scaling tracks the size
of the interactions (H3) is uninformative: the Spearman
correlation is +0.14, with interval [−1, +1].
How much does the assumed feedforward latency mat-
ter? Our plan called for repeating the extrapolations
with τff = 0.5 and 2 µs, and because the length of every
window that was run is recorded, this needs no new data.
On ibm marrakesh, tslot = 1.956 µs and τw = 6.868 µs,
so a different latency moves the zero-window target from
λL = 0 to λL = 1 −(3tslot + τff)/τw, that is, to +0.07 for
0.5 µs and to −0.15 for 2 µs. The joint and one-at-a-time
estimates then move by at most 0.032, or 0.046 for the
poorly determined GHZ spectator; the median biases of
the multi-knob strategies stay between 0.050 and 0.067,
against 0.126 to 0.142 for latency-only extrapolation; and
no interval, computed with the pre-specified bootstrap,
moves across the ideal value. Gate-only extrapolation
does not involve λL, and uniform scaling, which extrapo-
lates all three knobs along a single line in nominal units,

The feedforward observable fared differently. Its bLM,
which excluded zero in the main run, was +0.0017
[−0.0026, +0.0064] in the repeat run; in the follow-up
it was −0.0054 [−0.0096, −0.0013] in the unrotated cir-
cuits but +0.0014 [−0.0021, +0.0049] in the rotated ones,
which for this observable should be equivalent (Sec. IV B).
The like-for-like repeat in the follow-up thus matched the
main run, but the repeat run and the rotated circuits did
not, and we do not regard that interaction as consistently
reproduced.
E.
A check on the error bars
One choice in our plan deserves a second look. The
bootstrap resamples the twirled instances of each circuit
and then redraws the shots within each instance. But the
spread between instances already contains the shot noise,
so the second step counts it twice, and the intervals come
out too wide: here by 20 to 40%, except for the GHZ
spectator, whose values vary between instances far more
than shot noise alone would explain. As a check that was
not part of the plan, we reran our analysis code with only
that step removed, resampling whole instances, and with
two different random seeds. Resampling whole instances
is the usual bootstrap for data that come in clusters, and
we regard it as the better estimate of the uncertainties;

12
but because it was not in our plan, the pre-specified inter-
vals remain primary for the trial runs, main experiment,
comparison set, and first repeat throughout this paper,
and we use the narrower intervals to strengthen none of
our conclusions about them. (The plan for the follow-up,
written afterwards, adopted the instance-level bootstrap;
its main conclusion holds with either, Sec. IV D.)
The primary result does not change: bLM = +0.0007
[−0.0022, +0.0037]. Nor do the effect of the echo or the
comparison of extrapolation strategies, where no interval
moves across the ideal value. Three secondary statements
do change. Four of the 24 interaction coefficients now
exclude zero before correction, or five with one of the
seeds: besides the two above, bGLM for the probe’s feed-
forward observable (+0.0039 [+0.0008, +0.0071]), bGL for
the teleportation observable (+0.0111 [+0.0005, +0.0213])
and, marginally, bGLM for the GHZ spectator; about 1.2
would be expected by chance. The difference between the
repeat run and the main run, −0.0038 [−0.0073, −0.0003],
no longer contains zero, so H4 would fail by its own rule,
in line with the drift flag on the repeat run. And the
curvature now excludes zero at both centre points for the
teleportation spectator, and clearly so at (2, 2, 1) for the
GHZ parity.
F.
Does the latency knob stretch the window
faithfully?
Extrapolation to λL = 0 assumes that the window as
it is run, padding and feedforward wait included, decays
at the same rate as the free evolution that the latency
knob adds to stretch it.
If the window decays faster,
the extrapolation removes too little of its noise, and the
estimates come out low.
Two kinds of data test this
assumption for the probe: the shape of the latency axis,
which we analysed after the runs, outside our plan, and
the cost of the padding, which a trial run measured and
the follow-up measured again, as planned.
The first is the shape of the latency axis. Together, the
main experiment and comparison set ran every circuit
at five latency levels, λL = 1 to 5, with λG = λM =
1. For the probe spectator, ln⟨Xs⟩fell by 0.073, 0.054,
0.039 and 0.034 in successive steps: the decay slows as
the window grows, as the centre points had suggested.
A quadratic through the five levels has the curvature
kL = +0.0065 [+0.0028, +0.0098]. Because the five levels
come from two experimental runs, we use this coefficient,
which, unlike a contrast between three of the levels, is not
affected by a constant offset between the main experiment
and comparison set. The echoed trial run on the same
device, which ran all five levels in a single run, gives
+0.012 [+0.004, +0.019]. By Eq. (12), curvature of this
size shifts the multi-knob estimates by −3kL, that is, by
−0.020 or −0.035, against −0.043 (roughly −0.085 to
−0.001) from the centre points; curvature along λG and
λM would add to this. For the other observables the
scans add little. They show no curvature for the probe’s
feedforward observable, the teleportation observable or
the GHZ parity; for the GHZ spectator they agree in sign
with the centre points; and for the teleportation spectator
they show curvature of the opposite sign.
The second is the duration control, which measures
the cost of the padding directly, by running the circuit
with and without it. The trial run on ibm marrakesh did
this at one setting, and the follow-up at λL = 1 and 3,
with four times as many shots. In the trial run, the 2tslot
of padding lowered ⟨Xs⟩by 0.064 (Sec. IV A), that is,
ln⟨Xs⟩by 0.068 [0.050, 0.087], or 0.017 [0.013, 0.022] per
microsecond. In the main experiment, the free evolution
added by the latency knob cost only 0.0095 [0.0089, 0.0101]
per microsecond (−bL/τw). The padding, which is part of
the native window, thus decayed 1.8 [1.3, 2.4] times as fast
as the stretch. In the follow-up it cost 0.055 [0.049, 0.061]
at λL = 1, and decayed 1.5 [1.3, 1.7] times as fast as the
stretch of the same run. If each slot of padding costs p on
the logarithmic scale and the stretch costs r per unit time,
every multi-knob estimate falls short of the value without
a window by 3(p −rtslot), plus a similar term for the
feedforward wait, which we cannot vary (Appendix A). For
the spectator, the first term alone is 0.046 [0.019, 0.075]
from the trial run and 0.027 [0.017, 0.038] from the follow-
up. On ibm kingston, by contrast, the echoed padding
lowered ln⟨Xs⟩by only 0.007 [−0.007, 0.022], close to the
0.009 that the rate of the stretch there predicts. And for
the probe’s feedforward observable, the follow-up found
no excess at all: its padding decayed 0.97 [0.83, 1.11]
times as fast as its stretch, against 1.2 to 1.7 times in the
trial run, depending on which run supplies the rate of the
stretch.
For the spectator, the duration control directly estab-
lishes a higher average cost per unit time for the tested
padding than for the stretch. The latency scan indepen-
dently shows a nonconstant decay rate over the sampled
range.
Neither measurement separately identifies the
unvaried feedforward wait, and interpreting them as a
native-window mismatch requires the response assump-
tions stated above. For the feedforward observable the
evidence is mixed. They are two estimates of the same
shortfall, not corrections to be added. Under those mod-
els, the point estimates imply log shifts of 0.020 to 0.046,
compared with an observed log deficit of 0.067 to 0.084.
These are model-dependent scales, not an identified causal
fraction of the total bias: the estimates span different runs
and the wait contribution is unmeasured. The two more
precise point estimates are 0.020 from the main latency
scan and 0.027 from the follow-up duration control.
Why does the padding decay so fast? The simplest
explanation would be that the first microseconds of any
echoed delay decay fastest. That would also create an
interaction. If only the total length of echoed delay mat-
tered, the order within it would not: with one measure-
ment the stretch adds to the 2tslot of padding, whereas
with three it starts from no delay at all, and it would
then cost more. In that case bLM = (c3 −c1)/4, where
c1 and c3 are the costs of the padding at λL = 1 and 3

13
(Appendix A); and if, in addition, the decay is fastest at
first, c3 can be no larger than the cost 2rtslot of the same
length of stretch, so that bLM ≤−(p −rtslot)/2. Before
the follow-up we could test only this bound, taking p from
the trial run and r from the main experiment. For the
spectator it is −0.008 [−0.013, −0.003], whereas the main
experiment found +0.0007, which seemed to rule the expla-
nation out. The follow-up measured both costs in a single
run. At λL = 3 the padding cost 0.042 [0.031, 0.054]:
less than at λL = 1, but a little more than the 0.037
that the rate of the stretch implies. The total-length
prediction, −0.0031 [−0.0063, +0.0002], agrees with the
−0.0014 measured in the same run (difference +0.0017
[−0.0017, +0.0052]), and so does that for the feedforward
observable (−0.0026 [−0.0083, +0.0030], against −0.0054).
But the spectator’s measured value exceeds the bound
for a decay that is fastest at first, −0.0045, by 0.0031
[0.0003, 0.0058], or [−0.0007, +0.0070] with the bootstrap
of our original plan. The follow-up thus shrinks the earlier
contradiction, from 0.008 to 0.003, but does not clearly
remove it, and the difference between the two costs, 0.012
[−0.001, +0.025], is too uncertain to settle the matter:
why the padding decays so fast remains open. If decay
did depend only on the total length of delay, duration
matching itself would contribute about −0.003 to the
measured bLM of both probe observables. That would
cut both ways. It would make the feedforward observ-
able’s −0.0056 in the main run, together with the spread
between runs (Sec. IV D), weak evidence that latency and
measurement noise interact physically; and it would put
the spectator’s interaction in the main run, net of this
artifact, at +0.0038 [−0.0024, +0.0103] with the boot-
strap of our original plan, or [−0.0006, +0.0085] with the
instance-level one. The bound of Sec. IV B applies to the
knobs as built, duration matching included.
G.
Precision and an additive full-cube fit
For a post-hoc comparison of estimator precision, we
resampled the 20 complete twirled instances within each
setting, independently across settings, using 20,000 repli-
cates and seed 20261012. All 20,000 replicates were re-
tained for each observable under the E > 0.05 guard.
This post-hoc analysis assumes exchangeable instances
within a setting and does not model unobserved temporal
correlation. It preserves the original outcomes and does
not replace the pre-specified intervals. Figure 6 shows
the resulting estimator intervals and the separate analytic
shot-cost comparison.
The additive cube fit gives 0.941 [0.931, 0.951] for the
probe spectator, 0.969 [0.958, 0.980] for probe feedforward,
0.976 [0.942, 1.016] for teleportation, 1.004 [0.989, 1.019]
for its spectator, and 0.819 [0.771, 0.870] for GHZ parity.
The GHZ spectator is a counterexample to adopting it in-
discriminately: its additive estimate is 0.597 [0.498, 0.711],
whereas the original joint point estimate is 0.970. Its triple
contrast, bGLM = −0.0473, contributes strongly through
Eq. (9). Fitting only main effects therefore trades vari-
ance for potentially large misspecification error. Choosing
an estimator after looking at agreement with the known
answer would require independent validation; these six
outcomes do not establish an estimator-selection rule.
V.
DISCUSSION
What have we learned? Within the resolution of the
experiment, and once the idle periods are echoed, the pri-
mary interaction is consistent with zero: with the knobs as
built, the latency × measurement interaction seen by the
spectator is smaller than 0.005 on the logarithmic scale.
Elsewhere a few small interactions may be present. Two
of the 24 interaction coefficients exclude zero in the pre-
specified analysis, and four or five with the instance-level
bootstrap of Sec. IV E, against 1.2 expected by chance;
except for the poorly determined GHZ spectator, every
interaction is smaller in magnitude than the latency main
effect. Accordingly, the simple strategies, one knob at a
time or all knobs together, do about as well as the full
joint fit. For these circuits, this agrees with the argument
of Ref. [7] that a single global parameter can suffice, pro-
vided that every kind of noise that matters is amplified.
In the teleportation and GHZ circuits, amplifying latency
or gates alone left a much larger bias, which illustrates the
risk of leaving a source of noise unamplified; in Ref. [16],
for example, ZNE amplified gates and idling but not the
mid-circuit resets. Our single-knob strategies leave gate
or latency noise unamplified as well. In the probe, whose
measured gate-knob effect is small, latency-only extrapo-
lation did as well as any. Note also that uniform scaling
needs the same three knobs as component-resolved scaling;
what it saves is circuit settings, not instrumentation.
The clearest lesson may be methodological. Without
the echo, our first trial run showed a log-scale interaction
of 0.02, which could easily have been read as a physical ef-
fect; on the same device, the echo suppressed the rotation
and reduced the interaction to a value consistent with
zero. Carrera Vazquez et al. had already seen that coher-
ent oscillations spoil extrapolation in the latency [9]; what
Eq. (11) adds is that they also masquerade as interactions.
Indeed the effect is generic: whenever two knobs both
shift a coherent phase, the logarithm of the observable
acquires a cross term. Studies that look for interactions
between amplified noise channels should therefore refocus
static phases, or fit a model that allows for them, be-
fore interpreting the interactions. (Because the trial runs
shaped our design, this comparison is exploratory.) The
echo refocuses only the window, however. Phases picked
up during the measurement slots do not depend on λL,
so by Eq. (11) they can contribute to bGM, if gate folding
also shifts the phase, but not to bLM. Whether this ex-
plains the one bGM whose interval excludes zero, for the
teleportation spectator, we have not tested. The other
interval that excludes zero, bLM for the probe’s feedfor-
ward observable, did not recur consistently in later runs,

analysis recovered it at the size injected, at the cost of
about one more set of the eight corners. But the same
run carried a warning: two sets of circuits that should be
equivalent for the feedforward observable gave values of its
bLM that differ by 0.007, a difference that a permutation
test made after the run puts at p = 0.02 (Sec. IV B).
The measurement knob also departed from the textbook
picture. Three repetitions raised the parity error by a
factor of about 1.5 rather than 3, and later repetitions
erred more often than earlier ones. Extrapolation along
λM assumes that each repetition adds the same amount
of noise; on this hardware it apparently does not, so the
effective amplification factor differs from the nominal one.
Why, then, do the multi-knob estimates still fall short
for half of the observables? We can offer three candidate
explanations. The simplest is the gap between E∗and
Eid: noise that no knob amplifies, such as that of single-
qubit gates, state preparation, terminal readout and the
echo pulses themselves. Readout alone is too small to
explain the probe’s deficit, however; the reported readout
errors of its qubits, 0.0020 to 0.0046, would lower ⟨Xs⟩by
only about 0.004 and ⟨ZtZa⟩by about 0.018. A second
candidate, and for the probe spectator the best supported,
is that the latency knob does not stretch the window faith-
fully. As Sec. IV F showed, the tested spectator padding
has a higher average decay cost than the added stretch,
and the latency scan is curved, with model-dependent log
shifts comparable to part of the observed deficit. This
does not identify a causal share of the shortfall. A non-
constant decay rate is one possible explanation, but the
specific fastest-at-first model is in mild tension with one
of the post-hoc intervals and is not established. For the
GHZ parity, though, the latency scan shows no curvature,
the two centre points disagree in sign, and we have no
duration control for that circuit; curvature along λG and
λM we could not measure at all. A third candidate is
the measurement knob itself. If it amplifies measurement
errors by less than its nominal factor on ibm marrakesh
too (we measured this only on ibm kingston), extrapola-
tion along λM removes only part of the feedforward error,
which biases low the observables that depend on correc-
tions but not the spectators; the teleportation observable,
however, shows no such bias. Finally, the retargeting
sensitivity analysis within the assumed range does not
remove the shortfalls: changing it from 1 to 0.5 or 2 µs
moves the probe’s multi-knob estimates by less than 0.01
and the GHZ parity’s by less than 0.025 (Sec. IV C).
Agreement with the ideal value is not proof of correct-
ness either. The teleportation spectator sits on qubit 9,
whose reported readout error is 0.0156. No knob amplifies
readout error, and because our terminal measurements are
twirled, it acts as a symmetric bit flip, so by itself it should

15
cap the extrapolated ⟨X⟩at about 1 −2 × 0.0156 ≈0.969.
Yet the multi-knob estimates are 1.002 to 1.004, above the
cap: clearly so for uniform scaling, only just for one-at-
a-time, and within the uncertainty for the joint fit. The
positive curvature of this observable at both centre points
would push the estimates upward, so the extrapolations
may over-correct here; but the latency scan shows curva-
ture of the opposite sign (Sec. IV F), and the cap rests on
a single calibration value, so the evidence is weak.
Our study has several limitations. The analysis was not
registered externally, and the trial runs shaped the design.
The main experiment ran on a different device from two
of the trial runs. The circuits are small, with at most
eight qubits, and come from a single family of devices.
The measurement knob has only two levels and the gate
knob’s centre level is a mixture, so curvature along either
cannot be seen. The extrapolations to λL = 0 depend on
the assumed feedforward latency, which we varied only
over the range our plan specified, 0.5 to 2 µs. The padding
needed for duration matching raises the baseline idle noise,
and on ibm marrakesh it decays faster than the stretch,
which our extrapolations do not correct; the two runs
give model-dependent contributions of 0.046 and 0.027
to the log deficit. And folding lengthens the idle time
before the measurement, which we did not compensate.
Both repeat runs were flagged for drift, and the reported
calibration values may not describe the device at the time
of the runs (Appendix B). With two levels per knob, our
interactions describe non-additivity between the levels
tested, not the whole response surface. Our decision rule
for the primary interaction in the main run compared its
uncertainty with the minimum detectable effect rather
than testing equivalence; the test of equivalence in the
follow-up used a margin fixed in advance, but chosen
after the main run had produced it. The positive control
injects a coherent interaction after the window; it shows
that the analysis recovers an interaction of known size
on this hardware, not that every physical mechanism
of that size would appear in the same way. Two sets
of circuits that should be equivalent for one observable
gave values of its bLM that differ by 0.007 (Sec. IV B).
And our bound on bLM applies to the knobs as built: if
echoed delay decays according to its total length, part of
any bLM measured with duration matching is an artifact
of the design (Sec. IV F). Above all, a small primary
coefficient supports only a small value of that contrast at
the tested settings. Observable separability requires all
relevant mixed contrasts to be small; accurate mitigation
additionally requires response and target assumptions.
VI.
CONCLUSION
A factorial experiment can diagnose the observable
response to separately implemented noise-amplification
knobs, but it cannot by itself certify a zero-noise estimate.
The primary gate-averaged latency–measurement contrast
is small on the echoed probe, and the planned follow-up
meets its stated equivalence criterion while recovering
injected interactions. The result concerns the knobs as
implemented, including duration matching. It does not
establish independence of physical channels, equivalence
of every conditional contrast, or stability between calibra-
tions.
The distinction matters experimentally: all five original
estimators miss the known ideal value for three of six
observables. Padding controls and latency scans reveal
departures from a faithful scaling coordinate, while the
two-level measurement design leaves its zero coordinate
unidentifiable without further assumptions. A full joint
fit pays a substantial variance cost, and the additive full-
cube fit illustrates how reducing that cost can expose
model bias. Neither complexity nor a small interaction
coefficient is an accuracy guarantee.
A stronger next experiment would use additional phys-
ically realized amplification levels, an independently cali-
brated latency origin, contemporaneous padding controls
for each circuit, and repeated randomized blocks spanning
calibration changes. It should pre-specify an error toler-
ance for the total estimator-relevant contrast and allocate
shots according to the chosen estimator’s variance, be-
fore measuring ideal-value agreement on withheld circuits.
The experiment establishes a reproducible diagnostic case
study and identifies what those further measurements
must resolve.

The text quotes a few values from the calibration snap-
shots recorded at submission.
For the main run, the
reported terminal-readout errors were 0.0020 to 0.0046 on
the probe’s qubits (4–6 on ibm marrakesh) and 0.0156
on qubit 9, which carries the teleportation spectator. For
the repeat run, T1 and T2 changed, notably on qubit 8 (T1
from 214 to 33 µs and T2 from 148 to 59 µs), which the
probe does not use; but the reported readout and gate
errors were identical to those of the main run, so they
may not have been refreshed. Before the follow-up, the
reported readout errors of qubits 4 and 5 were 0.0081 and
0.0096, against 0.0046 and 0.0043 for the main run; the
error of mid-circuit measurements on qubit 5 was 0.0186,
against 0.0020; and the reported CZ errors had fallen by
about a third. The readout error reported for qubit 11
(0.2708) is inconsistent with our data: the GHZ spectator
measured on that qubit gave ⟨X⟩= 0.6215 in the native
circuit, whereas a twirled readout error of 0.2708 alone
would cap it at 1 −2 × 0.2708 ≈0.46. We do not use that
value. The full snapshots are part of the data set.
