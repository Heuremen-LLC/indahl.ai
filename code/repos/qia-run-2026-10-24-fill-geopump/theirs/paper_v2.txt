Cornaglia Affiliation: Centro Atómico Bariloche, Instituto de Nanociencia y Nanotecnología CONICET-CNEA and Instituto Balseiro (8400), San Carlos de Bariloche, Argentina Abstract Geometric heat pumping is the transfer of energy between reservoirs at equal temperature where the heat exchanged in an adiabatic cycle depends only on the driving contour in parameter space. We implement this mechanism on a superconducting quantum processor using a collision model, where a driven qubit interacts repeatedly with two ancilla qubits that are prepared in thermal states and emulate the reservoirs. Energy measurements before and after each interaction provide a calorimetric record of the heat exchanged with each reservoir. By reversing the driving contour, we extract a heat contribution associated with pumping. Its dependence on the period and on the contour follows exact simulations of the collision model, without adjustable parameters, and approaches in the slow-driving regime the geometric limit of the corresponding Lindblad dynamics. Introduction. Two reservoirs at the same temperature exchange no net heat. Yet a system coupled in an asymmetric way to both reservoirs and driven around a closed loop in parameter space can transfer heat from one to the other. This is the thermal counterpart of quantum particle pumping, in which a periodic modulation generates transport without a bias, a mechanism experimentally realized in electronic systems [ 39 , 45 , 54 , 63 , 34 , 57 , 32 , 19 , 43 ] and also explored with cold atoms [ 5 , 30 , 4 ] . In the adiabatic regime, where driving is slow compared with relaxation [ 50 ] , the number of pumped particles per cycle is characterized by a line integral along the contour defined by the periodic driving parameters, analogous to a Berry phase [ 16 , 8 , 7 , 67 , 14 ] . Similar geometric concepts also provide powerful tools for designing and characterizing fast periodic protocols [ 64 , 37 ] . In systems with nontrivial topology, the pumped charge can furthermore become quantized [ 51 ] . Adiabatic quantum heat pumping and its geometric characterization have also been investigated theoretically in driven-qubit systems [ 6 , 15 , 66 , 25 ] . Examples include pumping induced by driven thermal baths [ 56 ] , which is similar to pumping in classical systems [ 60 ] , as well as power transfer between driving sources [ 49 , 36 ] . When the reservoirs have different temperatures, pumping enables the heat–work conversion underlying continuously operated heat engines and refrigerators [ 48 , 15 , 66 , 25 ] . Unlike discrete-stroke thermal machines [ 58 , 68 , 3 , 23 ] , these devices remain coupled to both reservoirs and must operate against a continuous heat leak, as do thermoelectric machines [ 13 ] and adiabatically driven electron motors under voltage bias [ 21 , 20 ] . The connection between heat pumping and refrigeration is particularly relevant for quantum processors, where efficient qubit cooling is essential for accurate operation. Many proposals use auxiliary qubits as a thermal machine that cools a target while dissipating heat elsewhere, motivating bounds on the achievable cooling [ 31 , 12 , 69 ] . Complementary approaches rely on measurements [ 18 , 17 , 61 ] , device architecture [ 53 , 2 , 11 ] , or geometric optimization of finite-time dissipation [ 65 ] . Experimental realizations of quantum thermal machines [ 58 , 68 , 2 ] were an important breakthrough in the development of the field of quantum thermodynamics [ 22 ] . They have established the feasibility of controlling energy changes in few-level systems coupled to effective baths. Geometric heat pumping poses a distinct experimental challenge: resolving a contribution governed by the driving contour while distinguishing it from dissipative heating. This requires control over the driving protocol and reservoir interactions, together with measurements of the heat exchanged with each reservoir. Despite recent experimental progress, the measurement of heat remains particularly challenging in quantum devices [ 52 ] . Programmable quantum processors provide suitable capabilities for the implementation of driving protocols, and thermal reservoirs by repeated interactions with auxiliary qubits [ 38 , 26 , 35 ] in a quantum collision model [ 29 , 27 ] . Each ancilla is prepared in a thermal state, interacts with the working system, and is then reset before being reused. Measurements before and after the interaction determine its energy change, allowing the ancilla to serve as both a reservoir unit and a calorimeter for the implemented model. Repeating this sequence provides a microscopic description of Markovian open-system dynamics and consistent thermodynamic bookkeeping for heat, work, and nonequilibrium steady states [ 44 , 62 , 9 , 59 , 40 , 33 , 10 ] . In this Letter, we use this approach to implement geometric heat pumping on a superconducting quantum processor. A working qubit interacts with two ancillas representing reservoirs at equal temperature. Periodic modulation changes both the qubit level splitting and its relative coupling to the reservoirs. In the adiabatic regime, the qubit absorbs heat as its splitting decreases and releases heat as it increases; coupling these stages preferentially to different reservoirs produces directed heat transport. By reversing the driving contour, we isolate an orientation-odd heat contribution and characterize its dependence on the contour and driving period. Comparison with the discrete collision model and its continuous-time Lindblad limit connects the measured response to geometric pumping, even in the presence of a larger heating background. As expected, the odd heat grows with the area of the loop and vanishes within errors at δ B = 0 \delta_{B}=0 , where the area is zero. Summary and conclusions. We have demonstrated geometric heat pumping on a quantum processor by cyclically driving a working qubit asymmetrically coupled to two ancillas. Repeated ancilla interactions emulate thermal reservoirs and a net energy transfer between them takes place in the cycle. Although we focused on equal reservoir temperatures, the pumping remains robust against a small thermal bias in the numerical simulations in the intermediate 𝒯 \mathcal{T} regime (not shown). In the present implementation, noise-induced heating exceeds the pumped heat, precluding net cooling. Although Eq. ( 16 ) cancels the orientation-even background in the measured estimator, it does not remove the physical heating. Circuit-level noise simulations identify gate errors and relaxation and dephasing as important contributions, with the working qubit particularly affected during the waiting intervals associated with ancilla measurement and reset. Reducing these waiting times, improving gate fidelities, and increasing coherence times are therefore promising routes towards net heat extraction. An interesting direction is using more than one working qubit to generate the pumping [ 25 ] . These results establish a proof of concept for geometric control of heat flow, and ultimately cooling qubits, through programmable driving protocols.
