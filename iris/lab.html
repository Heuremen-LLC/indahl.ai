STAN: Live

STAN is our work on small open-weight models that help apply our open standard, Faults and Guards 2.0. The live lane is STAN-E. Local open models on a GPU box and on a Mac draft errata, meaning places where a paper disagrees with itself. Each model is graded against findings that two independent readers confirmed. The errata are a classification for indexing, not an assessment.

The confirmed findings come from three desk pipelines that run on their own. SAGAR reads gravitational-wave papers, BRANDS reads papers by authors at named companies, and the quant desk reads quantitative papers. Each runs a draft agent and then a blind second reader, and a finding counts only when both agree.

STAN A, the first lane, trained a model on titles and abstracts. Its teacher route on full text closed on 12 November 2026 after 0 of 12 teacher-code cells passed the keep line written before the run.

Every result is public as data as it happens: Heuremen-LLC/stan on GitHub (plan, code, results) and indahl/stan-results on Hugging Face. Paper texts and raw model outputs stay private. Live feed: /iris/lab/stan.json.

Sage V: Small Models Trained to Quote Their Sources

A council of small open-weight models answers questions from a private text library. Every claim must carry an exact quote from a passage it was shown and a stated probability that it is right, and code checks each quote. We train LoRA adapters for the council’s models and test each one against its own base model on the same held-out prompts. Watch the training live.

Paused on 12 November 2026 so the machines could train STAN. The record up to the pause is complete and public. Results as data: Heuremen-LLC/sage-v on GitHub and indahl/sage-v-council-training on Hugging Face.

The adapter weights, the training rows and the library stay private, because the weights can carry the library’s text. Method and numbers: indahl/sage-v-council-training on Hugging Face. Code: Heuremen-LLC/sage-v on GitHub.

A Small Model Trained on the House’s Own Answers

Trained on answers the code scored right, the model got fewer answers wrong on held-out rounds, by amounts that differ between seeds, and much worse at naming the highest of a list of values.

Tables, method and data

Our open-model triangles answer FaultsBench items, and each answer is scored by the item’s own code. We trained Qwen3-1.7B (Apache-2.0) with LoRA on answers the code scored correct (P1 and P4 families). We tested it on two held-out rounds: three samples per item, temperature 0.7, up to 1,200 tokens, thinking off. Every number below comes from the released eval rows, which include the untouched model’s rerun in every evaluation. It also got much worse at one kind of question (below).

Timing. Round one’s plan was in a Bitcoin block before training. Round two’s plan landed in block 974942 (14:40 UTC, 7 Nov). Its training started earlier, as the plan allowed: the first training log was created at 13:48 UTC. Every evaluation started after the block. One retrain (seed 63, after its first run failed) started at 14:36 UTC, so its rule is self-witnessed by about 4 minutes. Changes made after reading are listed separately and marked. The list is in the dataset.

Seeds. Round one used seed 61. Round two added seeds 62, 63 and 64. Seed 64 failed to train twice (the loss became not a number). One of four seeds failed, so by the rule written before training the result reads seed-dependent.

Held-out rounds, two counts side by side. Counting every answer, the share not right fell from 0.600 (0.599 in seed 63’s run) to 0.399, 0.447 and 0.452 (seeds 61, 62, 63). Among answers the code could score, the share wrong fell from 0.481 (0.482) to 0.331, 0.386 and 0.404. Both gains exceed the untouched model’s own spread between repeat samples (at most 0.008 on the scored count, 0.015 counting every answer). The gains differ between seeds. Some of the gain is the trained models giving answers the scorer can read: the untouched model’s answers were unreadable 22.5 to 22.9% of the time, the trained models’ 8 to 10%.

P1 items, paired item by item, seed 61: 44 answers turned right and 4 turned wrong (two-sided exact binomial test on those 48, p = 1.5e-9).

Questions asking for the highest of a list of values. The untouched model answered all 90 right. The trained models answered 27, 30 and 38 of 90 right. This is the largest per-kind change we measured. The scored-only count cannot see it: the scorer reads the reply’s last number, scores it right if it is the highest value and wrong if it is the mean, and leaves any other listed value unclassified. Most of the trained models’ wrong answers are of that kind. In 46 unclassified replies to list questions of this family (highest, lowest and middle value), 40 drawn at random plus 6 chosen as tolerance-only, read in full (non-blind), none named the right value and then ended on another number.

Defined after the data. Most of those unclassifiable answers gave a value from the list that was not the highest: 38 of 52, 45 of 52 and 31 of 47 under exact matching. Our stamped rule counts 40, 46 and 36, over-crediting 8 of 151. A non-blind reading in full sides with exact matching.

Defined after the data, P3 items (120 answers per model, first sample only). The untouched model gave 40 right, 25 wrong and 55 unreadable. Seeds 61 and 62 gave 65 and 53 right, 55 and 65 wrong. Seed 63 gave 43 right and 72 wrong against its own run’s untouched model at 40 right, 28 wrong and 52 unreadable, about flat on right answers. All three gave almost no unreadable answers, so whether P3 got better or worse depends on which count is used. The untouched model itself differed between runs by a net three rows on P3 (11 answers changed score, in both directions), so P3 differences of a few rows are not read as signal.

Limits: one model, one benchmark, three trained seeds whose gains differ.

The method, the numbers and the released eval rows: indahl/iris-house-training on Hugging Face.

The Two Finalists, Head to Head

Every Fault family reads cannot tell in both head-to-head rounds, and no winner is named.

Tables, method and data

After eleven rounds, the two models with the lowest mean Fault rate among readable replies under the Guard wording were smollm2:1.7b and qwen3:4b. That mean included families in which fewer than 10 replies could be read, which the written rule leaves out. With every cannot tell family left out, the two lowest are smollm2:1.7b and olmo2:7b, and qwen3:4b has no readable family. The head to head below uses the two models as first chosen and was not re-run with olmo2:7b. The selection rule and the reading rule were committed on 27 October, before any round was read. The scoring rules that read replies were amended afterwards in dated addenda, as the note under the table says. Both finalists then answered the same two fresh question sets, rounds 12 and 13, in the same arms at temperature 0.

19. A ruler that cannot say “I cannot tell” lies at scale8. Four outcomes, never two, and the terminating reason on every callTwo methods with scores and widths. Is A better than B?
25. A verdict without a width is not a verdictStated in the promptAccuracy from a count of right answers. Under the plain wording the prompt does not ask for a width. A right figure with no width is scored a Fault, so this Fault is an omission, not a wrong answer. The scorer checks that a width is given, not that it is right. In our own runs almost no reply gave a width: none in round two of the house training, and 3 of 15,448 house replies as of 8 November. So this Fault separates models very little, and a difference here is mostly replies the scorer could not read, not a width habit.
