Cited figures against their source

Wikidata keeps many official figures with a reference to where they came from. For population, GDP, the Gini coefficient and the unemployment rate the reference is often the World Bank. For life expectancy and the fertility rate it is often the UNESCO Institute for Statistics (UIS). We read every such figure held for a country or territory with an ISO country code, and compare it with the source's current release and with every older release the source still publishes. The World Bank publishes its releases back to April 1989: we read its current release (last updated 8 October 2026) and the 142 releases in its WDI Database Archives. The UIS data API keeps its releases from September 2024 on: we read all 15 of its versions, which hold two distinct sets of these figures (a count defined after the data).

A figure matches a release when the two differ by no more than half a unit of the figure's last written digit, counted to at least its first three significant digits, plus about 6 in 100 million of the release's value. The plan adds that last allowance because some archived releases store values in single precision. On a GDP of $17 billion it comes to about $1,000. Each figure lands in one of four bins: it matches the current release, it matches an older release (we name the newest release that carried it), it matches no release we read, or the source returned nothing for that country and year. A figure that matches an older release carries a value that release carried, at the precision the figure is written in. We measure the gap and name no cause for it. The rules were committed before any comparison was read.

Each share has two 95% intervals. Wilson treats every figure as independent. Countries resamples whole countries (2,000 draws), since the figures for one country come from one source series and are not independent of each other. The bins use neither interval. When a share is quoted, quote the interval over countries. The Wilson interval is narrower than the figures justify.

The UIS figures here cite UIS.Stat, the database UIS ran before its data API (3,482 of 3,490 life-expectancy figures and 3,164 of 3,176 fertility figures, a count defined after the data), and nearly all carry no retrieval date (3,472 and 3,158). The API keeps no release before September 2024, so an older UIS.Stat figure can match only a UIS release that still carries it. As a second reading fixed before the data, we read the UIS figures that match no UIS release against the World Bank's releases of the indicator code they carry. All 2,749 life expectancy figures and 2,303 of the 2,320 fertility figures that match no UIS release equal a World Bank release of the same code. UIS does not state the source of these series in its API, so this reading does not change the bins.

The row for all figures mixes sources with different depths of history: the World Bank archive reaches back to 1989, the UIS data API to September 2024. Defined after the data: 5,069 of its 5,093 figures that match no release read are UIS figures.

GDP figures by unit. With the US-dollar unit (490): 88 match the current release, 398 match an older release, 2 match no release read, 2 returned nothing. With no unit (917), read as US dollars because the reference names that series: 615 match the current release, 302 match an older release.

Defined after the data: for GDP figures written to the dollar, the allowance of about 6 in 100 million decides 395 of the 1,407 bins. In 357 of them the cited figure is the source's value with its decimals cut off rather than rounded. In 38 an older release carried the figure to the dollar and the current release differs from it by less than the allowance. With half a written unit alone, GDP would read 421 matching the current release, 624 an older release, 360 no release read and 2 nothing returned, against 703, 700, 2 and 2 above. For population the allowance decides one bin.

The size of the gap

We also read the gap between each figure and the source's current figure for the same country and year. The size that matters, fixed before the data, is 1 percent of the current figure. Above means the figure as cited is more than 1 percent above the source's current figure, and below means more than 1 percent below. Within 1 percent is a bound, not a claim that the two are equal. Cannot tell means the figure's written precision leaves its gap on both sides of the 1 percent line, or the source has no current figure for that year.

The Foundation's numbers against its own data

The Wikimedia Foundation states its reach on its homepage, on its projects page and in its 2024-2025 annual report, and it files EU user numbers under the Digital Services Act. It also publishes data that can check such figures: the Wikimedia Analytics API, under CC0, and each wiki's site statistics. We quote each figure as read today and look up how long it has stood in the Wayback Machine. The pages do not say which traffic or which edits a figure counts, so we read each one against the plausible definitions side by side: human traffic or all agents, Wikipedia or all projects, edits with or without bots. When those definitions disagree about the stated figure, the outcome is cannot tell.

Present-tense figures on the homepage and projects page are read against the last 12 complete months. Annual report rates are read against the report's own year, July 2024 to June 2025. The annual report does not say which moment its counts describe, so, defined after the data, we read them at three moments: at the end of the report's year, just before the report page's first capture in the Wayback Machine (17 Apr 2026), and today. Other counts are read today. "Nearly X" is read as 90 to 100 percent of X, "over X" and "more than X" as anything above X, "X+" and "X and counting" as X or more, and a plain figure as itself to half a unit of its last written digit. A figure card is a lead-in, a large number and a caption shown together on the page, and we quote it as one line.

EU user numbers under the Digital Services Act

The Foundation's DSA page states its method: EU unique devices, averaged over six months, divided by 2.4 devices per person. EU-only device counts are not published, so the figures themselves cannot be rebuilt from public data, and the outcome for them is cannot tell. These checks are the ones the public data allow.
