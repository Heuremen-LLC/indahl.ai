IBM’s calibration, measured when it changes

We run one job on each IBM processor we can reach whenever IBM publishes new readout or T1 figures for it, and at least every 6 hours, and read every qubit: readout at rest, readout after an X, and T1. The first runs on 30 October went every hour. In about 40 hours of IBM records, IBM changed those figures between 0 and 9 times per processor, so most hourly runs read a record that had not changed, and the rule moved to plan Note 4 the same day. Each reading is set against the calibration IBM had in force when our job ran, with both figures and their dates. If your job ran near one of ours, this is what your qubits were doing. One job per processor is kept in flight, so a processor with a job waiting in IBM’s queue shows its older reading beside the time the newer job was sent. The two interval rules were set after the first aachen run was read and before any US run was read. A third rule, the shot count behind IBM’s readout figures (1,024 on ibm_fez and ibm_marrakesh, 4,096 elsewhere), was set on 30 October after fez and marrakesh readings were published, and every earlier fez and marrakesh run was re-read under it, each correction added to the chain. The code and the plan are public: qia-cal.

When IBM’s calibration was off: the chain

Every time one of our own jobs measured a qubit against the calibration IBM published for it and found IBM’s figure outside what the qubit delivered, the reading goes here as one entry: processor, qubits, IBM’s figure and the date IBM gives for it, what we measured with its interval, the time our job ran, and the job id. Check your own job: pick the processor, type your qubits and the time your job ran. Entries are hashed and chained, append only, so the record cannot change without showing it. Every calibration run above adds one entry too. Everything is free: the whole chain and the script that verifies it.

IBM’s published calibration, checked against itself

We read the calibration IBM publishes for each processor and check every record against itself. No QPU time is used. The rules were committed before the fresh read and the counts were recomputed by a second script that shares no code with the first. Boston qubit 85 and fez qubit 148 were noticed in a first look before the rules were written, and the two and a half threshold was set after that look. Every other row was read under the committed rules.

A single qubit cannot have T2 above twice its T1. A qubit is marked above when T2 is more than two and a half times T1. It is marked cannot tell when the ratio is between two and two and a half, because IBM publishes no fit uncertainty. We also compare the date IBM gives for each qubit’s T1 with the date it gives for its T2, the age of every entry in the record, and whether a restamped record changed any qubit entry at all. We name no cause.

To measure T1 and T2 together on a flagged qubit yourself, with your own IBM account, use the check in our public repository. It prints its cost first and submits nothing until you say so.
