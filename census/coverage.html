CENSUS · The Census Count Against Its Own Survey

6 areas BELOW, 8 ABOVE, 37 CONSISTENT, 0 CANNOT_TELL

The Census Bureau measured the 2020 count of each state against its own independent survey. At the 90 percent level that Census states, the 50 states and the District of Columbia are each read one of three ways, and CANNOT_TELL is kept as its own count (none here).

Division: CENSUS

Where each state count sits against the Census Bureau’s own survey

This product uses the Census Bureau Data API but is not endorsed or certified by the Census Bureau.

After the 2020 Census the Census Bureau ran the Post-Enumeration Survey. It is an independent sample survey whose people are matched case by case to the census. From it Census publishes a net coverage error for each state with a standard error. Net coverage error is the census count minus the survey estimate, as a percent. A negative figure is an undercount and a positive figure is an overcount. The survey is the Census Bureau’s own measure of its own count. This page does not judge the count. It sets each published count beside the width of the second measurement and reads where the count sits.

Who the survey covers

The survey covers the household population of the 50 states and the District of Columbia on 1 April 2020. It leaves out people living in group quarters such as nursing homes, college dormitories, jails, shelters and military barracks, and people living in Remote Alaska areas. The census count inside the survey tables is therefore a household count rounded to the thousand, and it is smaller than the resident total. Both are in the table. The gap between them is group quarters and Remote Alaska and is not an undercount figure. The reading applies to the household count. Puerto Rico is published in a separate table and is not read here.

The reading rule

Three terms first. The standard error is how far the survey figure could move from sampling alone. Nonsampling error is anything that moves a figure other than sampling noise, for example people matched incorrectly, and the standard error leaves it out. A 90 percent interval is the range that holds the true value 9 times in 10 if the survey method is unbiased. Census states that survey comparisons are tested at the 90 percent confidence level with a two sided test, and its state charts show a 90 percent confidence interval around each estimate. We compute the interval as the net coverage error plus or minus 1.645 times its standard error. This uses the 90 percent level Census states. The lowest flagged ratio of net coverage error to standard error is 1.93 (Delaware) and the highest unflagged is 1.58 (Louisiana), so no multiplier from 1.59 to 1.93 changes the 14 flagged areas. An area reads BELOW when the whole interval is under zero, so the census count sits below the survey estimate. It reads ABOVE when the whole interval is over zero. It reads CONSISTENT when the interval holds zero. It reads CANNOT_TELL when no width is published. The four outcomes are counted apart and CANNOT_TELL is never folded into another count. The rule was written down and sealed before any count of outcomes was computed. The raw data and one look at the full list came before the seal, see the end.

BELOW means the census count missed people, an undercount. ABOVE means the census count was more than the people there, an overcount. CONSISTENT means the interval holds zero.

The United States

For the nation the survey gives a net coverage error of -0.24 percent with a standard error of 0.25, so the 90 percent interval is -0.65 to 0.17 percent and the national cell reads CONSISTENT. This is its own cell and is not a count of areas.

A second method, Demographic Analysis

Demographic Analysis is a different method. It builds the population from births, deaths, migration and Medicare records and does not use a survey. It covers the total resident population, and the survey covers the household population outside Remote Alaska, so the two are not the same people and are never merged here. Census gives three Demographic Analysis series for 2020. The low series is a net coverage error of 0.22 percent and is an overcount, the middle series is -0.35 percent and is an undercount, and the high series is -1.21 percent and is an undercount. The three series are scenarios that Census gives and not a statistical interval, and Census states no standard error for them, so this cell reads the range and not an interval. The range holds zero. The survey national estimate of -0.24 percent sits inside that range.

Omissions and erroneous enumerations

Census also publishes the pieces behind the net figure for each state. Omissions are people the census missed. Erroneous enumerations are people counted that should not have been, here split into duplicates and other reasons. Each value is shown in percent with its standard error, and the national value is the first row. These are values only. Census rounds them to one decimal place and a rounded value cannot carry a reading, so we do not read them against the national value or against the net reading above. Census does not publish a total of erroneous enumerations by state, so none is formed.

What this page holds and what it leaves out

It holds the state figures of one Census Bureau release, the Post-Enumeration Survey results of 2022, and the Demographic Analysis release of 10 March 2022. Nothing on it refreshes. It leaves out regions, race, age and housing units, Puerto Rico, any cause, and any effect on apportionment or funding.

What came before the seal. We pulled the raw data before the plan was written and sealed. We also printed the full list of survey figures once, with a quick marker at 1.645 times the standard error, and it matched the areas Census names. So the match with the Census asterisks is a replication and not a prediction. No count of outcomes or page existed then. The seal covers the text of the plan and the rule and nothing else. The code was committed after the first reading. The first run failed two of its own checks. Our parser missed counts under one million, which turned every outcome into CANNOT_TELL, so no outcome came from the bad parser. We fixed the parser and the rule did not change.
