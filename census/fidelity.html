Does the CDC count of divorces and marriages in a state sit inside the Census estimate?

This product uses the Census Bureau Data API but is not endorsed or certified by the Census Bureau.

The CDC and the Census Bureau count different things, so a gap between them is a reading of that difference and not a claim that either number is mistaken. CDC NCHS counts the events that states report, where they happened. The ACS estimates the persons who say they married or divorced in the last year, where they live now.

The women's reading is the scored one and was fixed in the plan before the comparison. The men's counts are shown beside it. Massachusetts, Iowa and Kansas hold under one sex and not the other. Their divorce counts sit below the women's interval in 13 of 13, 12 of 13 and 12 of 13 of their scored years and below the men's in 4, 9 and 9, so they read below under the women's estimate and not under the men's.

In 2022 the NCHS total for the reporting states was 0.80 of the ACS women's total and 0.89 of the men's for divorces, and 0.89 of the women's and 0.85 of the men's for marriages. The two sources do not count the same thing, so a state sits against that ratio and not against 1.0. These readings cannot show that any state's records are missing or that either source is mistaken. They show that the two counts differ by more than the survey's stated margin.

What each source counts

NCHS publishes one count of marriages and one of divorces and annulments for each state that reports, by state of occurrence. NCHS says of its rate tables: “Rates are based on provisional counts of divorces by state of occurrence.” The divorce table carries the footnotes “Includes annulments.” and “Includes divorce petitions filed or legal separations for some counties or States.” The marriage table says “Rates are based on provisional counts of marriages by state of occurrence.” and “Marriage data includes nonlicensed marriages registered.” California, Hawaii, Indiana, Minnesota and New Mexico do not report divorce counts in some or all years of the frame, and Georgia and Louisiana have blank runs. The blanks in the frame years are California in all frame years, Hawaii in all frame years, Indiana in all frame years, Minnesota in all frame years, Georgia 2010 to 2016, Louisiana 2010 to 2012 and New Mexico 2016 to 2023. Georgia has no marriage count for 2013 and 2014. The ACS 1-year tables B12501 and B12503 give persons 15 years and over who married or divorced in the last 12 months, with a 90 percent margin. The last 12 months roll before each interview and are not the calendar year. NCHS counts are by place of the event, ACS by place of residence.

How we turned persons into events

Each marriage or divorce between a woman and a man has one woman in it, so we read the women's ACS estimate as the number of events and put its published 90 percent margin on it as the interval. We chose the women's estimate before the full comparison but not before seeing any data. A scout of 16 state-year-events had already shown the women's and the men's estimates for Texas, Florida, New York and Pennsylvania, and the plan lists them. The ACS pulls for all years came in the minute before the plan was committed and were not read before it. The code that applies the plan's rule was committed after the first comparison. The rule it applies is the one in the plan text, and an independent recount from the plan text equals every reading. The reader gate's rate tolerance first ran at 0.15 and was reset to the plan's 0.1 before any reading was written. The men's estimate is shown beside the women's in every headline line and is not scored. Rows where the women's and the men's intervals give different readings are counted apart as sex splits: 246 divorce rows and 85 marriage rows. A marriage or divorce of two women is counted twice by the women's estimate, which overcounts it, and one of two men is not counted at all, which undercounts it. The net sign is not known. The 95 percent scale of the margin is computed for display and does not set any outcome.

Occurrence against residence

This is the main limit. A couple can marry or divorce in a state where they do not live, and NCHS counts the event where it happened while ACS counts the person where they live. We name Nevada for both events and Hawaii for marriage as destination states and print them apart. For marriages these destination rows are 26 above and 0 inside or below, and for divorces 6 above and 7 inside. Every other state has some of the same crossing, so the other rows are not free of it. Florida, New York and Arkansas marriages also read above in most scored years (12 of 13, 10 of 13 and 10 of 13), so the destination states are not the only rows above.

Notes on the NCHS series

The Louisiana divorce count NCHS prints falls from 9,995 in 2021 to 3,393 in 2022 and 4,120 in 2023, and the printed rate falls with it. The Georgia count falls from 36,464 in 2017 to 26,327 in 2018. NCHS prints these counts, so the rows stay scored as printed and none is re-read.

In 12 divorce state-years of 2016 and 2017 the NCHS count table and the NCHS rate table do not agree to within 0.1 per 1,000 (Illinois 2016, Iowa 2016, Kansas 2016, Maine 2016, Maryland 2016, Massachusetts 2016, Texas 2016, Iowa 2017, Kansas 2017, Louisiana 2017, Maine 2017, Maryland 2017). The counts for Iowa, Kansas, Louisiana, Maine, Maryland and Massachusetts fit both years' printed rates when the 2016 and 2017 counts are swapped. Texas 2016 also differs, and NCHS footnotes the Texas 2016 divorce rate as revised. Illinois 2016 differs as well and is not footnoted for divorces. Every outcome here is read from the count table as printed. Under the swap 3 rows would change reading: Kansas 2017 from inside to below, Maine 2017 from inside to below and Massachusetts 2016 from below to inside. No row has been re-read.

Checks that gate every reading

Four state-years were read in full from the NCHS tables and the ACS summary files and equal the parsed rows: Texas 2022 divorces, Florida 2023 divorces, New York 2022 marriages and Pennsylvania 2023 marriages. The NCHS state counts add up to the printed United States figure in every year of both tables. NCHS counts over ACS population round to the printed NCHS rate within 0.1 in 1,183 of 1,242 state-years. The 59 that miss are listed in data/gate2_rate_misses.csv in the public desk folder. ACS API values equal the Census table-based summary files in 1,020 of 1,020 values for 2022 and 2023. The rule passed synthetic rows for each outcome. A failed check would have turned every reading into cannot tell. Gates passed in this run: yes.

What this does not say

A row above or below names no cause. NCHS labels its rate tables as based on provisional counts, and we keep a dated copy of the NCHS tables from 2026-11-11 so a provisional against revised reading becomes possible later. ACS estimates are a sample, and consecutive years of a state and the two sexes share that sample, so rows are not independent and we print no share or test over rows. The margin covers the ACS sampling only. It does not cover the NCHS side or the conversion from persons to events, so an inside reading means not distinguishable at this margin. NCHS divorce counts include annulments and in some states petitions. 2020 is left out as the Census did not publish standard 1-year estimates for it.
